Rethinking Running: Are We Training the Wrong Thing?

Rethinking Running: Are We Training the Wrong Thing? By Sam.

The cycle I keep seeing

I had a conversation recently with a woman who'd been told to stop running.

She'd been through rehab, done her strength work and tried to get back into running, but kept having problems. Eventually she was told that running was simply too hard on her body.

I was told something similar in my twenties, so I found this particularly frustrating.

And I keep meeting runners in much the same position.

They've spent months, sometimes years, working on their strength. They've seen physios, followed rehab programs, taken breaks and gradually built their running back up.

But the same problems keep returning. Calves, Achilles, knees, hips, lower backs.

Often they're quite strong. Some are experienced marathon and ultra runners who've already developed a lot of physical capacity.

Yet their running still feels difficult, and they're constantly managing something.

I've been thinking about why this happens, and whether we're sometimes spending too much time trying to improve our bodies before learning to use them better.

Why strength doesn't change how you run

I think we tend to approach running problems from the perspective of physical capacity.

If something keeps getting overloaded, we assume it needs to get stronger.

And there's plenty of truth in that. Strength, tendon capacity, fitness and appropriate training loads all matter.

But there's another part of running that I think gets overlooked.

How are you actually using the strength you've already got?

You can spend months strengthening your glutes without necessarily changing how you use them when you run.

You might still rely heavily on your quads, hamstrings or calves, because that's how your body has learned to organise the movement.

Getting stronger might allow you to tolerate more load, which is useful, but it doesn't automatically teach your body a different movement pattern.

I've been exploring this distinction between capacity and movement skill for quite a while now.

And the more runners I work with, the more interested I become in what happens when we focus on coordination rather than simply adding more strength.

Muscle running and spring running

Are your legs doing more work than they need to?

In my latest video, I describe two ways of approaching running.

I call them muscle running and spring running.

Muscle running is the way I see a lot of people approach movement. The legs are doing most of the obvious work. Muscles contract to push the body forward, and running starts to feel like a continuous series of muscular efforts.

Spring running is about making better use of the body's elastic properties.

Our muscles can create stiffness while our tendons stretch and recoil. The whole body can contribute to the movement, with the feet, hips, pelvis and torso working together.

Running always involves both muscular work and elastic energy. These aren't two completely separate ways the body functions.

But I think the distinction is useful because it helps us explore how efficiently we're using what we've got.

I demonstrate this with a pogo stick in the video.

A pogo stick doesn't need an engine to bounce. It needs the right structure, stiffness and spring. Once it's bouncing, leaning allows it to move forward.

Our bodies are obviously more complicated, but there's something worth understanding in that example.

When we organise our movement differently, we can change how force is produced, absorbed and returned.

And sometimes people feel that difference almost immediately.

They haven't got stronger in those few minutes. They've found another way to move.

I've put together a video demonstrating these ideas, including a few simple movement experiments that help explain the difference between using your muscles to push and using your body as a spring.

Why I've changed how I teach

This has probably been the biggest change in my coaching over the last year.

I used to spend a lot more time teaching the mechanics of running. Foot position, knee angles, hip extension, push-off and so on.

I still think those things are important, but I've learned that asking someone to change their mechanics before they've developed the underlying movement skills can create problems.

Someone might understand the instruction but use completely different muscles to achieve it.

That's why I'm now spending much more time on the foundations.

Can you feel your glutes working? Can you engage your core while keeping your legs relaxed? Can you move your hips and torso together rather than trying to do everything with your legs?

I particularly like working on walking, because we can slow things down and explore how the whole body contributes without immediately adding the demands of running.

Then we can gradually introduce leaning, bouncing and more challenging movements.

I'm finding that people often learn much more when they can feel the difference for themselves, rather than simply being told where to put their feet or how to move their knees.

And it's one of the reasons I'm increasingly focused on live coaching. I can see what people are doing, ask what they're feeling and help them adjust when something isn't quite working.

There's still a lot I'm learning about the best way to teach all of this, but I'm much happier with this approach.

A free seven-day challenge

I'm going to be exploring some of these ideas in my next YouTube Live, where I'll also be launching a free seven-day movement challenge.

The idea is pretty simple.

Over seven days, we'll spend around ten minutes a day exploring how to connect the glutes, core and torso, and gradually bring that connection into walking.

We'll do a little assessment at the beginning and repeat it at the end to see what changes.

I'm not suggesting that seven days will fix your running or resolve an injury. It's an opportunity to start noticing how you're using your body and experiment with some different movement patterns.

If you've spent years getting stronger but still feel like your legs are doing all the work, I'd love you to join me.

I'm interested to hear what you discover.
