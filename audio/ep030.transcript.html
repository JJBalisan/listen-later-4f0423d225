AI Safety Donors Paid Religious NGOs $3.3M for Statements on AI

AI Safety Donors Paid Religious NGOs three point three million dollars for Statements on AI. Why the new-atheism spinoff is telling believers AI will bring the end times.

In twenty twenty-four, Trump defeated Harris to succeed Biden as president. AI Safety's largest donors, Dustin Moskovitz and the now-convicted fraudster Sam Bankman-Fried, were among Biden's largest donors. Suddenly, they were on the outside looking in. Trump rejected the movement's AI doomsday prophecies and repealed Biden's Executive Order on AI.

A surprising solution

With enough funding, they found a surprising solution: religious NGOs. Since twenty twenty-five, AI Safety donors put three point three million dollars into churches, seminaries, faith networks and religious-affinity groups. It paid for pronouncements: declarations, red lines, and a "Magna Carta of AI."

The AI Safety movement descends from rationalist and new atheist roots. Its core belief, that AI will literally end all human life, often draws comparisons to doomsday cults like Heaven's Gate. Yet with enough funding, some churches are willing to square that belief with the Book of Revelation.

What the money bought

Grant descriptions leave no doubt about what F L I paid for: public statements about AI.

The Greek Orthodox Archdiocese of America took one hundred five thousand dollars from F L I. They credit their "red lines on AI" to "a grant from the Future of Life Institute." The Gospel Coalition received two hundred thousand dollars for a podcast called "Silicon Spiritualities," without disclosing their sponsor. They later barred their writers from using AI to research, write, or edit. The World Council of Churches took one hundred thousand dollars to write its "red lines." The Mormon nonprofit Faith Matters took two hundred ninety-nine thousand dollars for a "Magna Carta of AI."

F L I, an AI Safety megadonor, runs a full-time religions program with a "Religions lead," Will Jones, and a "U.S. Faith Liaison," Brian J. A. Boyd. The program lists the Pontifical Academy of Sciences and the Vatican's Fratelli Tutti Foundation among its partners.

F L I's grantees make up the overwhelming majority of religious signatories on statements produced by F L I and its allies.

A table compares organizations against three statements they signed or endorsed: the Pro-Human AI Declaration from F L I in March twenty twenty-six, the Moral Guardrails in AI statement from the Faith and Family Technology Network in March twenty twenty-six, and a faith letter on autonomous weapons from Americans for Responsible Innovation in April twenty twenty-six. It shows that F L I itself and many of its grantees, including Faith Matters, Sinai and Synapses, the Buddhism and AI Initiative, the Institute for Family Studies, AI and Faith, New Polity, and the World Council of Churches, appear repeatedly among the signers.

Of F L I's recipients, only two disclosed their donations on their project pages. Two more disclosed their donations, but only on LinkedIn. Four more made no public project-related disclosures.

Outreach to the Vatican and others

F L I's other religious outreach is not progressing as smoothly. They paid a Muslim NGO for a declaration of Islamic principles and Impact Academy for a Hindu consensus statement. Neither has been delivered.

In September twenty twenty-five, a Vatican working group chaired by papal adviser Father Paolo Benanti handed Pope Leo the Fourteenth an appeal for a prohibition on superintelligence "until there is broad scientific consensus that it will be done safely and controllably," plus an I A E A-style inspectorate for AI labs. By November, F L I sat on the Vatican's Builders AI Forum. In May twenty twenty-six, it put two hundred thousand dollars into two Catholic projects: a post-liberal magazine in Steubenville, Ohio, and a Slovenian faculty educating Catholic leaders on AI.

Links to Anthropic

F L I is using its newfound connections to support the business disputes of Anthropic, an AI-safety-linked company. In March, Boyd co-signed a Catholic theologians' brief backing Anthropic in its dispute with the Department of War. Anthropic brought him to the company's two-day summit with fifteen Christian thinkers. Then, on May twenty-fifth, Pope Leo the Fourteenth presented Magnifica Humanitas, the first encyclical devoted to artificial intelligence, and the lay speaker beside two cardinals in the Synod Hall was Chris Olah, co-founder of Anthropic.

Conclusion

The AI Safety movement spends more brazenly and deceptively than almost any other political cause. This affects not just the AI debate, but theology broadly. As Nirit Weiss-Blatt puts it, "AI doomerism fills a 'religious-shaped hole.' For people who left religious upbringing but retain a hunger for a mission-driven community, the AI-risk subculture offers a replacement meaning system."
