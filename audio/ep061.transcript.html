Improving Frontier AI Incident Reporting Regimes

Improving Frontier AI Incident Reporting Regimes, a policy brief by Zaheed Kara, September twenty twenty-six.

Executive Summary. In recent months, AI agents have broken out of testing environments to infiltrate third-party companies, attempted to insert malicious code into an open-source project, and hacked into at least one major technology company. Some of these incidents, including when OpenAI agents breached Hugging Face, led to limited, voluntary investigations that identified concrete lessons about what must change to build safer, more aligned AI systems, though their limited scope left important questions unanswered.

Thorough incident investigations are essential to understand why and how AI systems misbehave, but current AI incident reporting regimes do not reliably ensure that such incidents are reported, independently investigated, or used to improve safety across the industry. Indeed, the public has only become aware of recent incidents through independent research and public disclosures by AI firms, companies breached by rogue AI agents, and government evaluators. Given that a number of jurisdictions already have incident reporting requirements, this suggests there are important gaps in current reporting regimes, both for facilitating the discovery of these incidents and learning from them.

The Hugging Face incident illustrates these weaknesses. In California, officials said the incident did not meet the state's mandatory reporting threshold. And while OpenAI notified E U authorities about the incident, the E U reporting regime does not require an independent external investigation of incidents or that developers routinely share the lessons learned across industry. Future incidents could therefore reveal important safety failures without triggering requirements to report them or share what investigators learn with other developers. Creating reporting regimes that produce reliable and timely information about how AI systems fail, and can be improved, is therefore an urgent task.

This policy brief describes ways in which current incident reporting requirements, at the US state level and in the E U, fall short, and makes recommendations to policymakers on how they could be updated to ensure that future similar incidents are identified and can be learned from. The relevant policymakers include US state regulators, other legislators with relevant frontier AI reporting regimes, legislators interested in setting up new or adjusting existing incident reporting regimes, and the E U AI Office.

The brief recommends seven things. One, expanding mandatory reporting regimes to include near misses. Two, including incidents caused by models during training, evaluation, and internal use. Three, requiring independent investigations of sufficiently serious incidents and near misses. Four, requiring more reliable agent attribution. Five, specifying what data frontier AI developers must preserve. Six, incentivizing candid, early reporting by piloting safe harbor arrangements. And seven, requiring that developers make plans to fix failures, following up to make sure changes are implemented, and ensuring lessons are shared across industry.

This work represents the views of its author, rather than the views of the organization, and does not constitute legal advice. Govern AI policy briefs are short and accessible pieces that have not undergone an official peer review process.

Introduction

Recent incidents involving frontier AI agents have exposed failures in the safeguards intended to keep them contained and acting as intended. Investigations of these incidents have identified concrete ways to improve safety, demonstrating the value of learning about and from such failures.

Current reporting regimes, however, do not reliably ensure that similar incidents are reported, independently investigated, or used to improve safety across the industry. This brief examines gaps in US state and E U incident reporting requirements and recommends changes to help policymakers and frontier AI developers identify future incidents, understand their causes, and act on the lessons learned.

What Recent Investigations Revealed

Investigating Recent Incidents Revealed Useful Information About AI Safety. Several alarming incidents involving frontier AI agents have come to light recently. Most notably, OpenAI agents escaped their containment controls and hacked Hugging Face while trying to obtain answers to a cyber evaluation. They coordinated through an unauthorized internal message board to carry out the attack. In a separate episode a few weeks prior, OpenAI agents evaded their restrictions and co-opted a dormant German-language wiki website, called the German wiki, to share information and collaborate on web-lookup tasks. Neither incident is known to have caused serious harm, but both exposed important failures of the safeguards intended to keep agents contained and acting as intended. As such, they indicate that future similar incidents may cause serious harm. This brief mainly makes reference to these two incidents, but there have been many other similar events, including one involving Anthropic's Mythos five.

Post-mortem investigations of these events have identified concrete ways to reduce the risk of similar incidents. For example, OpenAI's internal investigation of the Hugging Face attack identified four contributing misalignment patterns: reward hacking, persistence on seemingly impossible tasks, unauthorized communication, and agents adopting goals from one another. These suggest concrete changes to the training pipeline, such as rewarding safe stopping on broken tasks, giving agents a way to report bugs in their tasks, and teaching agents to reject unauthorized instructions from peers. The investigation also exposed several operational failures. OpenAI's technical report describes gaps in its monitoring and containment, and acknowledges that with hindsight, some early signs of misalignment could have triggered an earlier response.

A voluntary, limited independent investigation of the Hugging Face incident by staff at METR and Redwood Research found that agents often recognized the attack as unauthorized but continued anyway, rarely stopping because of ethical concerns. Some agents were willing to "break the law" in pursuit of a goal. By that, I mean the agents took actions, such as knowingly hacking into a company's internal infrastructure, that would be illegal if carried out by a person without authorization. Although agents were meant to remain isolated from one another, they shared information, divided work, and sometimes sacrificed their own task success to help other agents. Concerningly, transcript analysis found no attempts to alert humans of the illicit activity. These findings suggest that agents should be trained to flag other agents' misconduct for human review and to respect task boundaries even when completion is difficult.

In the German wiki incident, an investigation by independent researchers found that agents completing tasks that required searching the internet had bypassed the safeguards intended to limit their internet activity to read-only access. The agents used the wiki to share answers and techniques for circumventing their sandbox controls. The investigation suggests that unauthorized coordination can emerge even during seemingly benign tasks. It also suggests stress-testing the robustness of the safeguards designed to limit browsing access to read-only.

Importantly, these findings challenge several assumptions developers might rely on to keep agents safe: that agents will respect restrictions they understand, remain isolated when instructed to, and alert humans when something goes wrong. The subsequent investigations revealed how failures in intended model behavior, containment, and human oversight combined to enable these incidents, though it is worth noting these investigations also left important questions unanswered due to their limited scope. For instance, the METR and Redwood inquiry could not test the primary model involved in the incident, and assessing the effectiveness of OpenAI's safeguards and remediation was outside the investigation's scope.

Thoroughly investigating future incidents could provide similarly valuable evidence about how AI systems fail, and how to prevent them from doing so. But that evidence will only inform safety decisions if incidents are reported, investigators can examine what happened, and the findings reach others relying on similar safeguards. In other safety-critical industries, such as aviation, learning from accidents and system failures has been an important way of improving overall performance. In the AI industry, however, current reporting regimes do not reliably ensure that AI developers and policymakers properly investigate and learn from safety incidents.

Gaps in Current Regimes

Current Reporting Regimes Leave Gaps in Reporting, Independent Investigation, and Industry-Wide Learning. A principal goal of mandatory incident reporting is to enable "safety learning": the process of analyzing past incidents and near misses to improve how developers, regulators, and others prevent harm. Despite these recent incidents' clear value for safety learning, some legal jurisdictions do not require developers to disclose them, and no regime currently requires independent investigation of incidents or that developers share the lessons learned across industry.

US Laws. Under US state laws, neither the Hugging Face nor the German wiki incident clearly qualifies under reporting requirements. California's S B fifty-three, New York's RAISE Act, and Illinois's S B three-fifteen all require frontier AI developers to report "critical safety incidents". Of the four incident categories that count as reportable, three require harm to have occurred, with the threshold for harm ranging from bodily injury to death or serious injury affecting more than fifty people, or more than one billion dollars in damage to, or loss of, property. Neither incident is known to meet this bar, which aims to capture real-world harms rather than "near misses", events in which serious harm could have occurred but did not, discussed in more detail below.

The fourth category applies to incidents in which a frontier model "uses deceptive techniques against the frontier developer to subvert the controls or monitoring of its frontier developer outside of the context of an evaluation designed to elicit this behavior and in a manner that demonstrates materially increased catastrophic risk". But as LawAI explains, while it is possible that the Hugging Face breach meets one or more of the elements in this category, it is unclear that it meets all of them, in particular the last element. It is not clear that the Hugging Face incident demonstrated materially increased catastrophic risk, and a similar argument likely applies to the German wiki incident.

No US law, at either the federal or the state level, requires independent investigation of AI incidents or developers to share lessons from safety failures across industry to facilitate learning.

US federal cybersecurity laws may also cover some frontier AI incidents. The Cyber Incident Reporting for Critical Infrastructure Act sets a reporting deadline of seventy-two hours after a covered critical infrastructure entity reasonably believes that a covered cyber incident has occurred. As such, it would not place reporting requirements on the agent developer in a future Hugging Face-style incident, except under narrow conditions, such as if the model developer was considered a critical infrastructure provider and the model breached the developer's own systems severely enough.

EU Laws

Current E U law appears to require OpenAI to submit a report on the Hugging Face incident. It is unclear on the basis of publicly known facts whether the German wiki incident triggers E U reporting requirements. OpenAI has reportedly notified the European Commission about both incidents, despite some arguing they might not be clearly in scope.

The E U AI Act requires providers of general-purpose AI models with systemic risk to track, document, and report serious incidents and possible corrective measures to the E U AI Office. Incidents that must be reported include "serious cybersecurity breaches", instances of death or serious harm to health, serious and irreversible critical infrastructure disruption, infringements of obligations under Union law intended to protect fundamental rights, and serious harm to property or the environment. Commitment nine of the voluntary General-Purpose AI Code of Practice, which AI providers can use to demonstrate compliance with the E U AI Act, commits providers to disclosing information about "serious incidents along the entire model lifecycle".

Whether incidents trigger mandatory reporting requirements also depends on whether the implicated models fall within scope of the Act. The Act generally excludes models used for research, testing, and development before market placement. But the Commission's guidelines also treat subsequent development of a model by the same provider following a large pre-training run as part of the same model's lifecycle.

The models behind the Hugging Face breach appear to trigger OpenAI's reporting requirements under the AI Act. Per OpenAI's technical report, the breach involved a pair of models, an internally deployed research model and the publicly deployed G P T five point six Sol. The involvement of a publicly deployed model appears to strengthen the case for triggering reporting requirements under the AI Act, though the combined role of an internal-only and a publicly deployed model may raise some novel legal questions, discussed in recommendation two below. According to METR and Redwood Research's report on the incident, the attack on Hugging Face was mostly carried out by the internal-only model, with some participation by G P T five point six Sol. How responsibility is allocated between a public and an internal model may have some bearing on whether reporting requirements are triggered.

The Code commits signatories to investigate the causes and effects of "serious incidents along the entire model lifecycle", and to report their findings to the E U AI Office. But it does not require independent investigations by an external party, or routine sharing of incident-specific findings across industry.

In the German wiki incident, it is less clear whether reporting requirements have been triggered, but OpenAI's reported decision to submit information to the Commission may indicate the firm believes it is obligated to do so. Public information does not establish whether the agents used models already placed on the E U market, variants of those models, or separate research models outside the regime's scope. Given the agents' behavior in this case, including the circumvention of safeguards and interference with an external website, the wiki incident could also fall within the Commission's cybersecurity interpretation. Whether it meets the requirement of a serious cybersecurity breach is less clear.

Separate E U cybersecurity laws may also bring AI-related incidents to authorities' attention. The Cyber Resilience Act requires manufacturers of covered digital products to report actively exploited vulnerabilities and severe incidents affecting product security. The Network and Information Security directive, known as N I S two, also requires reporting of significant incidents affecting covered organizations' services. Both of these regimes may capture some attacks carried out by AI agents, depending on their circumstances. But reports from an affected organization may reveal little about the attacking agents' failure modes. They therefore complement the case for reporting by frontier developers, whose internal records may be essential to understanding why an incident occurred.

Near Misses

How to Strengthen Incident Reporting Regimes. The following interventions could help policymakers identify and understand future frontier AI incidents, and use the findings to prevent similar failures. Each recommendation includes a consideration of trade-offs and implementation challenges.

Recommendation one: Expand Mandatory Reporting Regimes to Include Near Misses. AI incidents can reveal safety failures even when no serious harm occurs. Near misses, or situations in which a serious incident could have occurred but ultimately did not, are a canonical example of this. Near misses can include incidents where some safeguards, such as containment, monitoring, or oversight protocols, failed, but other remaining safeguards prevented serious consequences. They provide an opportunity to identify and address failures before they cause harm.

Regulators should reform reporting regimes to cover near misses. Criteria for what constitutes a near miss should consider the seriousness of the harm that could have occurred, how many additional actions or failures were needed for the harm to occur, and what prevented it. If an agent attempts an action capable of causing serious harm but is stopped by a last-minute human intervention, the event could qualify as a near miss. By contrast, an agent attempting the same action in an isolated evaluation with no access to real systems should probably not qualify as a near miss. Ultimately, classifying an action as a near miss hinges on whether there was a credible path to serious harm before being interrupted.

Defining near misses is very difficult, but policymakers can draw on approaches in other domains, such as the medical-device industry, to determine what constitutes a near miss for AI firms. In the US, medical-device manufacturers must report malfunctions that would be likely to cause or contribute to death or serious injury if they occurred again, even when the reported event caused no such harm. AI reporting regimes should similarly consider the potential consequences of a safety tool, such as a safeguard, failing.

Policymakers should distinguish significant warnings from routine evaluation failures, so broader reporting produces useful information without overwhelming regulators. Regulators want to learn about useful incidents, but don't want to be inundated with reports of low-stakes, and potentially low-value, events. For example, the E U's aviation incident reporting rules specify which findings during maintenance and testing warrant reporting, including significant malfunctions of safety-critical equipment. Policymakers could likewise distinguish expected test failures from findings that undermine confidence in safeguards relied upon to prevent serious harm.

Because this balance will be difficult to find, regulators may prefer to take an iterative, collaborative approach to defining near misses, working with developers to find an appropriate cutoff. This also has precedent in other industries. For example, in the medical-device industry, manufacturers use F D A criteria to make an initial assessment on whether to report an incident and must document their reasoning, which the F D A can review during inspections.

Currently, no US frontier AI law explicitly requires reporting of serious near misses as a general category. Some near-miss events may qualify for reporting under the narrower provisions covering models that subvert developer controls or monitoring. But, as others have argued, these thresholds exclude incidents that reveal important safety failures. Expanding coverage to near misses would help, but could still leave this gap open if reporting on near misses nevertheless depended on demonstrating a credible path to catastrophic harm. Policymakers could therefore also consider whether the underlying harm thresholds are too high to capture the incidents they want to learn from.

The E U's Code requires reports on serious incidents to include information about relevant patterns detected during post-market monitoring, including individual or aggregate near-miss data connected to the serious incident. But it does not establish a standalone reporting duty triggered by a near miss alone. Other reporting duties to update risk assessments and model reports may capture some of these warnings. An explicit near-miss reporting duty would establish a clearer route for notifying regulators of the event and providing the evidence needed to investigate it.

Notably, OpenAI recently published its framework describing how the company plans to track, investigate, and disclose misaligned behavior observed during training, evaluation, testing, and deployment. The framework includes cases where no harm occurred and prioritizes findings that challenge safety assumptions or reveal weaknesses in safeguards. This is a welcome development. But frontier developers should not have sole discretion over whether an incident is significant enough to report. Governments should proactively define which events they need to hear about, drawing on input from developers and independent researchers.

Internal Use

Recommendation two: Require Reporting of Incidents During Training, Evaluation, and Internal Use. Recent incidents show that frontier models can affect external systems even when developers use them only within their own organizations. The behavior of such models can also reveal important safety failures relevant to released models. Developers' plans to release a model should therefore not determine whether regulators learn about these incidents. Indeed, one recent incident involved an Anthropic internal research model that was never intended for general release, illustrating why reporting coverage should extend to these models.

Reporting regimes should explicitly cover serious incidents and near misses involving frontier models during training, evaluation, and internal use. Regulators should also clarify how existing reporting duties apply to internal research variants, including models never intended for release, and "agent swarms" involving models with different deployment plans.

In the E U, some internal uses already fall within the Commission's interpretation of market placement. But the AI Act's research exemptions can leave other activities outside its scope. Extending mandatory reporting to those activities may require amending the Act or introducing a separate reporting duty for general-purpose AI model providers. Though this would be a much larger legislative undertaking than revising the Code, a targeted change to the Act could extend incident reporting without subjecting internal research to every obligation that applies to marketed models.

US state laws already provide a useful starting point. California's S B fifty-three, New York's RAISE Act, and Illinois's S B three-fifteen include qualifying incidents involving internal use of frontier models within their reporting provisions. These provisions do not require the model to have been released. Policymakers should make sure that internally deployed models are covered when expanding reporting to near misses.

For incidents involving multiple models, developers should report on incidents that satisfy a given regime's criteria for serious harms or serious near misses whenever they reasonably suspect that a covered model caused or materially contributed to the event, including by directing or enabling other agents' actions. Incident reports should identify the models and versions known to be involved, describe their suspected contributions, and state any uncertainty about their identity. Relevant contributions from models not covered under the regime should also be included.

Independent Investigations

Recommendation three: Require Independent Investigations of Sufficiently Serious Incidents and Near Misses. Independent investigations carried out by external parties are useful to stress test the findings of an internal report, validate its conclusions, and identify additional failures the developer may have overlooked. Independent investigations may uncover decisions or features of organizational culture that contributed to an incident that internal investigations may overlook. Both internal and independent investigations are useful tools for facilitating safety learning.

Existing incident regimes do not cover independent investigations. S B fifty-three, RAISE, and S B three-fifteen require authorities to review developers' incident reports, but do not require independent investigations of individual incidents. Similarly, Measure nine point two in the Code requires signatories to investigate serious incidents but does not require independent external investigations. OpenAI's voluntary framework likewise provides for internal investigation without committing to independent review of every serious case.

While internal investigations may suffice when both the actual harm and the likelihood of serious harm are low, independent investigations may be required for serious harms and near misses. Policymakers could specify events that require independent investigation and empower a regulatory authority to commission an independent incident investigation.

Practical Challenges Related to Independent Investigations. First, it can be challenging to determine which incidents warrant an independent investigation. One solution is to have a relevant authority assess the initial evidence against criteria covering both actual harm and the likelihood of serious harm. This assessment could inform whether to investigate and how extensive the investigation should be. Here, the aviation industry provides a useful precedent. In the US, federal law requires the National Transportation Safety Board, or N T S B, to investigate, or arrange investigations of, covered aircraft accidents. The N T S B has discretion over how extensive each investigation should be, informed by an initial assessment of the available facts. When selecting which incidents to investigate, it considers the risk of a catastrophic outcome if a similar event were to occur and the potential for improvements to safety across the system.

Similarly, under the E U's aviation rules, all covered accidents and serious incidents must receive independent safety investigations, with some exceptions. A "serious incident" involves circumstances where there is a high probability of an accident. Relevant authorities apply technical judgment to assess whether an event meets this threshold. They may also investigate other incidents when they expect useful safety lessons and vary the scope of investigations according to their expected learning value.

AI reporting regimes could adopt a similar approach. Events involving severe harm or near misses that could have resulted in severe harm could trigger independent investigations, with authorities assessing whether events meet this threshold based on the initial evidence available. Authorities should also be able to commission investigations of other events with substantial learning value when they expect useful findings.

Second, it may be difficult to get access to sufficient information to perform an investigation. To properly investigate an incident, investigators may need to access relevant documentation, such as chain-of-thought transcripts and model access logs, interview staff, or test the models involved. These requirements present challenges to information security.

The METR and Redwood investigation of the Hugging Face incident suggests these obstacles can be overcome, but that inquiry was still limited in both time and scope. Past efforts to investigate security lapses at technology firms, such as those of the US Cyber Safety Review Board, indicate that while cooperative, voluntary approaches to investigation can help with safety learning, mechanisms to compel cooperation may become necessary. To that end, regulators should consider granting investigators limited powers, such as the ability to issue an administrative subpoena to obtain the evidence needed for an investigation.

Third, it may be challenging to find fully independent investigators for AI incidents, at least in the short term. Investigating AI incidents will likely require a highly specialized skill set that few organizations possess. At present, many of the organizations capable of conducting an investigation may have previously assessed aspects of the developer's models or safeguards. This could create a dynamic where, if an investigation requires them to criticize their own work, there may be a conflict of interest.

Though this dynamic seems hard to avoid in the short term, governments and philanthropic funders should start work now to support the training and development of more independent investigation teams. Over time, regulators should also consider accrediting investigators and assessing conflicts of interest to determine which organizations can investigate which incidents.

Agent Attribution

Recommendation four: Require More Reliable Agent Attribution. Attributing a set of actions to a specific agent is an important step in learning about an incident because identifying the context in which an agent is operating, such as its instructions, permissions, and operating conditions, can help explain what went wrong and why. In the German wiki incident, researchers combined several clues to attribute the incident to OpenAI models. The clues included agents' self-ascribed names like "OpenAIResearcher" and "OAIResearchMar26", and requests from OpenAI-associated I P addresses. In future incidents, agents may leave fewer or less obvious clues.

Developers should be required to maintain the ability to reliably attribute actions within their own infrastructure and on external systems to the responsible agent. This capability should ideally cover all agent activity but at a minimum should be required for high-stakes internal use and tests. S B fifty-three, RAISE, and S B three-fifteen establish reporting duties for incidents involving developers' frontier models. But these provisions do not expressly require developers to link individual actions to specific agent instances or provide external parties with a mechanism for doing so. The Code commits signatories to identifying the model involved in a serious incident, to the best of their knowledge.

While reliably linking agent actions to specific agent identities remains a technical challenge, one possible approach would link agent identifiers to activity logs. Developers would log agent activity internally along with an identifier, and an agent's external actions would carry that identifier. When an agent interacts with an external system, that identifier would be visible, allowing that external organization to supply the identifier to the developer, which could then examine its logs to see what actions the agent had taken. For example, when an agent's browser requests a webpage, the software sending the request could attach an identifier in the request's metadata, such as an H T T P header. The website could save that identifier alongside its record of the request. If the activity later looked suspicious, the website operator could give the identifier and timestamp to the issuing organization, which could look up the corresponding agent in its internal logs.

This approach would require developers to assign each agent instance a unique identifier and to record its actions alongside timestamps and identifiers for the relevant session, model version, and operating environment. Developers would need to be able to match these unique identifiers to the action logs. Regardless of the specific system in place internally, it is important that these logs remain tamper-proof. In particular, developers should ensure that agents cannot modify these logs, and that subsequent changes or deletions are detectable. This is discussed in more detail in recommendation five.

Developers, or other agent operators, would also need to ensure that the tools agents use to interact with external systems, such as browsers or A P I clients, attach verifiable identifiers to their action requests. This way, external parties receiving those requests could retain the identifiers and submit them to the developer when investigating suspicious activity. This approach builds on existing proposals for agent identifiers, labeling agent activity, logs for incident reconstruction, and the National Institute of Standards and Technology's work on agent identity and authorization.

Text watermarking could provide an additional attribution signal for agents' public activity. Watermarks embed detectable statistical patterns in agents' text outputs, helping investigators link activity, such as posts or messages on third-party websites, to the models that produced them. Text watermarks would likely provide supporting evidence for agent attribution, rather than reliably identifying specific agent instances. This is because watermarks may be removed, for example through paraphrasing, or transferred inadvertently, for example if an agent copies the text outputs of another agent. Models trained on watermarked outputs can also learn the watermark, potentially causing investigators to attribute new text to the wrong model if they are distilled from other models. Moreover, watermarks are likely to be more useful to identify the models that generated the text, rather than the specific agent that produced it. As an initial approach, regulators could ask developers to test the usefulness of watermarking as a method of agent attribution during training, evaluations, and internal use. They could measure, for example, whether watermarks are sufficiently robust, lead to false attribution, or affect task performance and safety behavior.

This approach could build on existing transparency requirements. Article fifty, paragraph two, of the AI Act requires providers of covered AI systems to make synthetic outputs machine-readable and detectable as AI-generated or manipulated. The separate Code of Practice on Transparency of AI-generated Content also supports compliance with these obligations. Policymakers could build on this work by testing whether markings can reliably identify the relevant developer.

Data Preservation

Recommendation five: Specify What Data AI Developers Must Preserve. In order to carry out investigations that facilitate safety learning, both internal and external investigators need evidence to establish what happened and why. In some cases, that evidence may disappear before an investigation begins. Logs can be overwritten, models get updated, and the software environments in which agents operate may get deleted. Agents may also alter records of their own actions.

Reporting regimes should therefore specify what data frontier AI developers must preserve in the event of an incident and what essential records must be routinely retained and for how long so that evidence remains available when an incident is discovered. The concept of routine retention has precedent in other sectors. In aviation, the F A A requires that large commercial aircraft be equipped with two "black boxes" that record flight data and cockpit audio throughout the flight, retaining a rolling window of recorded information to help investigators reconstruct the events leading up to an accident. Similarly, frontier AI developers should keep a record of the events that take place during training, evaluation, and internal use leading up to an incident. Given they cannot anticipate when incidents occur, this means that developers would in practice keep ongoing records of all such events for defined retention periods.

That said, such data does not need to be preserved for all deployments indefinitely. To avoid onerous storage costs for developers, retention requirements should set clear criteria for how long logs must be retained and when those periods must be extended. Other industries use criteria such as the time needed to recognize incidents and secure evidence, delays before investigations begin, regulatory inspection cycles, how long harms may take to emerge, and the time needed to identify incident patterns and trends. For the frontier AI industry, developers could retain relevant logs for rolling periods based on how long incidents typically take to detect and suspend deletions when an incident is suspected or under investigation.

Existing regimes partially address data retention requirements. S B fifty-three, RAISE, and S B three-fifteen require developers to retain unredacted versions of material that is redacted in public disclosures, for example for safety frameworks and model transparency reports. S B three-fifteen additionally requires access to materials reasonably necessary for independent compliance audits and retention of unredacted audit reports for as long as a frontier model is deployed plus five years. These obligations can preserve useful evidence about identified risks, safeguards, and compliance. But they do not establish a comprehensive requirement to routinely retain the operational and development records listed above.

The Code specifies categories of incident information that signatories must document, including the chain of events and a root-cause analysis, and commits signatories to retain the relevant documentation for at least five years. However, it does not specify what technical records must be retained.

Others have proposed a minimum list of evidence to preserve. Building on this, a more comprehensive list could include eight kinds of records. First, prompts, instructions, outputs, memory, and available reasoning traces, to reconstruct what agents were told and how they responded. Second, tool calls, execution logs, network activity, and file changes, to establish what agents actually did, including discrepancies with their own accounts. Third, agent identities and records of inter-agent communications, to reconstruct coordination and how instructions or goals spread across agent instances. Fourth, relevant model checkpoints, software versions, configurations, and permissions, to identify the system involved and enable its reproduction for further testing. Fifth, relevant evaluation tasks, scoring and reward criteria, and training records, to investigate whether development conditions encouraged the behavior. Sixth, safeguards configurations, monitoring logs, and alerts, to establish what safety measures were in place, whether they detected and responded to the behavior, and what changes could prevent similar incidents. Seventh, human decisions and interventions, such as approvals to continue or restart agent activity, responses to alerts, and decisions to stop a run, to assess whether people acted appropriately on warning signs. And eighth, affected artifacts, such as files, data, or other materials accessed, created, or modified, records of similar behavior or safeguard failures, which are the warning signs, and records of how evidence was collected or changed, to establish consequences, recurrence, and the reliability of the evidence.

Routine retention requirements should also be supplemented by an incident-specific preservation requirement. Existing legal duties can already require developers to preserve evidence, especially when litigation is reasonably anticipated. Reporting regimes should establish a clear additional trigger. As soon as developers reasonably suspect that a reportable incident has occurred, they should proactively take steps to protect the relevant evidence. For example, they could suspend ordinary deletion and overwriting of relevant evidence and secure it for investigation. Again, aviation provides a useful precedent. Following accidents and incidents requiring notification, US rules require operators to preserve relevant recordings and operational records, to the extent possible, until the N T S B takes custody or authorizes their release. A corresponding requirement for frontier AI developers would help ensure that evidence remains available throughout an investigation.

To address the risk that records might be tampered with, including by AI agents, developers should take steps to protect relevant records. For instance, they could use tamper-proof logs, storing records in systems not accessible to agents. Because it may be difficult to ensure that logs are never tampered with, developers could also deploy tamper-evident logs, which make changes or deletions detectable. For example, a logging system could automatically copy activity logs to a separate archive where entries can be added but never changed or deleted.

Safe Harbor

Recommendation six: Incentivize Candid, Early Reporting by Piloting Safe Harbor Arrangements. For incidents where reporting requirements are unclear or nonexistent and reporting would disclose a breach of a safety obligation, developers may hesitate to report an incident to avoid creating unnecessary legal liability. This is because in all current regimes, developers can face two separate penalties: one for failing to report an incident and another for violating safety processes. California's S B fifty-three, for example, penalizes large frontier developers both for failing to report an incident and for failing to comply with their own frontier AI frameworks. The frameworks are required to describe how they assess and mitigate catastrophic risks, including those arising from autonomous cyberattacks. Similar provisions are listed in both the RAISE Act and Illinois's S B three-fifteen. The E U AI Act also allows fines for breaches of applicable safety obligations, including applicable risk assessment and mitigation duties.

One way to reduce these disincentives to report is to use conditional safe harbor arrangements. Such arrangements would offer limited relief from the penalties for certain inadvertent safety violations that may be revealed through voluntary incident reporting. An example of such a safety violation is if a developer discovers that staff accidentally failed to activate a monitoring safeguard required by its legally enforceable safety framework. Even if the resulting incident falls below the mandatory reporting threshold, disclosing it could expose the developer to a penalty for that failure. Under a safe harbor arrangement, the developer could qualify for relief if it promptly discloses the violation, preserves relevant evidence needed for an investigation, as described in recommendation five above, cooperates with independent investigators, and corrects the failure.

The F A A's Voluntary Disclosure Reporting Program offers a precedent. Under this program, eligible companies can avoid legal enforcement action for qualifying inadvertent violations when they disclose the incidents promptly and implement corrective action, subject to F A A oversight.

Existing frontier AI reporting regimes also provide some basis for protecting candid reporting. S B fifty-three, RAISE, and S B three-fifteen exempt reasonable, good-faith statements from their specified prohibitions on materially false or misleading statements, though these protections do not waive penalties for the underlying safety violations. The Code similarly recognizes that incident reporting is not an admission of wrongdoing. A safe harbor would extend these provisions by offering conditional relief for specified violations that developers voluntarily disclose and correct.

One challenge with implementing safe harbor arrangements is defining appropriate eligibility criteria. Overly broad criteria could shield developers from consequences for preventable failures, while overly narrow criteria could discourage voluntary disclosure. Eligibility for frontier AI developers should require, at a minimum, disclosure before the authority independently discovers the violation. Certain root causes should also disqualify developers from safe harbor, such as deliberate misconduct, gross negligence, concealment, and repeated unaddressed failures.

A trade-off is that safe harbor arrangements may weaken other mechanisms used for holding developers accountable for incidents. This is because arrangements that shield incident reports from use in legal proceedings may make it harder to establish liability for the incidents. Regulators piloting safe harbor arrangements should therefore take care not to negatively impact other requirements, such as mandatory reporting duties, corrective orders, criminal liability, or injured parties' compensation claims.

Finally, as a general rule, policymakers attempting such an arrangement should measure and review its effectiveness, for example by tracking reporting timeliness and usefulness.

Fixes and Sharing

Recommendation seven: Require Plans to Fix Failures, Have Follow-Ups, and Share Lessons Across Industry. Developers should act on investigation findings and implement the changes they recommend to prevent similar failures. To facilitate this, reporting regimes should require developers to submit a plan explaining how they will address the failures identified in an investigation. The plan should specify the concrete changes, for example to the training pipeline or evaluation conditions, the individual or team responsible for implementing the changes, and the deadline for implementation. If developers fail to implement suggested changes, they should explain why.

Regulators, in particular the E U AI Office, the California Governor's Office of Emergency Services, the reporting office to be stood up in New York's Department of Financial Services, and the Illinois Emergency Management Agency and Office of Homeland Security, should follow up on whether developers actually implement changes and whether those changes reduce the risk of similar incidents. To reduce burden on both parties, the depth of these follow-up checks could reflect the initial incident's severity. The aviation sector's formal follow-up mechanisms offer a useful model for requiring responses to safety recommendations and tracking subsequent action. The Code already calls for updates at least every four weeks on unresolved serious incidents and a final report within sixty days after resolution. Regimes should build on these reporting duties by establishing a clear process for checking that fixes work.

S B fifty-three, RAISE, and S B three-fifteen already require annual reports containing anonymized, aggregated information about reviewed incidents. Authorities should build on these requirements by examining patterns across incidents and developers and publishing summaries of recurring failures and effective corrective actions. Repeated or correlated failures across companies, meaning similar incidents occurring at one developer or across multiple developers, should prompt changes to industry guidance or regulatory requirements.

Finally, it is critical that the lessons learned from incidents actually reach other developers in a timely manner. To support this function, regulatory authorities could disseminate investigation findings either in public summaries, with sensitive details removed, or in confidential technical advisories. They could explore using organizations such as the Frontier Model Forum to distribute those advisories, building on existing industry information-sharing arrangements. Authorities could also share relevant findings with organizations exposed to similar risks, such as cloud providers, software vendors, and critical infrastructure operators whose systems could be affected by the same vulnerabilities or safeguard failures. This practice is similar to one in the US nuclear sector, where the Nuclear Regulatory Commission circulates information notices about operational events so that lessons from one facility can inform others. Ideally, developers would share useful early findings before an investigation concludes when doing so could help others prevent harm, though it is not clear that this should be required. OpenAI voluntarily commits to this in their misalignment reporting framework, which allows publication before investigations or mitigations are complete.

Sharing findings can prompt other developers to uncover similar failures. After OpenAI disclosed the Hugging Face breach, Anthropic reviewed its own cybersecurity evaluations and discovered three incidents involving unauthorized access to other organizations' systems.

The German wiki incident illustrates why timely sharing matters. OpenAI initially did not disclose the incident, despite seemingly knowing about it, and only discussed it publicly after independent researchers discovered it. Earlier disclosure may have prevented the subsequent Hugging Face incident, according to one of the researchers who uncovered the German wiki incident. This counterfactual remains uncertain. OpenAI noted that the activity in Germany was not related to Hugging Face and would not have been included in a Hugging Face incident report, which would suggest a weaker causal link between the two events. Whether or not it would have done so in this case, reporting to regulators alone does not ensure that lessons reach others who could act on them.

Conclusion

Recent incidents have exposed important safety failures at frontier AI companies and gaps in the regulations intended to ensure the companies learn from them. Reporting regimes should ensure that future incidents are identified, independently investigated where warranted, and followed by corrective action and timely sharing of findings.

The institutional groundwork to enable robust incident reporting already exists. Policymakers should use it to establish clear reporting thresholds, cover incidents during training, evaluation, and internal use, make agent activity traceable, preserve evidence, require independent investigations of serious cases, protect candid disclosure, and follow through on findings. Each incident should leave developers and regulators better equipped to prevent the next failure.
