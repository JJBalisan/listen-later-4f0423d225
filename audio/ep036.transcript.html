Process Matters more than Output for Distinguishing Humans from Machines

Process Matters more than Output for Distinguishing Humans from Machines.

Abstract. Reliable human-machine discrimination is becoming increasingly important as Large Language Models and autonomous agents are deployed in online settings. Existing approaches largely evaluate whether a system can produce behavior or responses indistinguishable from those of a human. This approach follows the focus on the output of a machine as a criterion for determining whether it can think, as suggested by Alan Turing. Cognitive science provides an alternative approach: considering the process by which that behavior is produced.

To evaluate whether differences in cognitive mechanisms can reliably distinguish humans from machines, we introduce a process-based framework, the Process Turing Test, and evaluate it across a battery of cognitive tasks spanning decision-making, working memory, and planning. These tasks, such as mental rotation and sequence prediction, yield process-level measures that reveal how behavior is generated, complementing conventional measures of overall task performance. We also include multiple CAPTCHA tasks in the battery to compare the experimental paradigms to challenges deployed in the real-world.

Across the battery, process-level features provide substantially stronger discriminative signal than performance metrics alone, reliably distinguishing humans from agents even when task performance is matched. The mean process-feature classifier AUC is zero point eight eight.

To assess agentic process limitations, we conduct a controlled red-teaming study comparing off-the-shelf frontier agents, namely Claude Sonnet four point five, GPT-5, and Gemini two point five Pro. We also include Centaur, a large language model fine-tuned on ten point seven million human decisions, and two task-specific fine-tuning methods applied to models of different sizes and families. These are action-level supervised fine-tuning, called A-SFT, and process-level fine-tuning, called P-SFT, which directly optimizes process features.

We find that broad fine-tuning on human choices makes task processes more human-like relative to off-the-shelf frontier agents. Task-specific process-level fine-tuning further improves human-like behavioral mimicry, though this advantage largely disappears under cross-task transfer when process targets do not naturally generalize across tasks. These results highlight process specification as a central bottleneck in achieving human-like cognitive processes in machines.

Introduction

Reliable human-machine discrimination is becoming increasingly important as large language models and autonomous agents are deployed in online settings, creating new challenges for security, fraud prevention, and platform integrity. Existing approaches to distinguishing humans from machines are largely task performance-based, evaluating whether a system can produce behavior or responses indistinguishable from those of a human. This paradigm underlies systems such as CAPTCHA-style challenge-response tests and automated content detectors, and reflects the enduring influence of the Turing Test, which answered "Can machines think?" by testing whether machines could produce human-like output.

Modern AI systems increasingly satisfy this output-based bar. GPT-4.5 is judged human seventy-three percent of the time in controlled Turing Tests, frontier vision models can solve reCAPTCHA version two challenges, and specialized AI systems now match or exceed human experts in domains once considered canonical tests of intelligence, including chess and Go. As AI systems approach human-level performance on many output-based evaluations, output is an increasingly weak criterion for distinguishing humans and machines.

When humans and AI systems produce similar task outputs, the latent processes generating those outputs may differ substantially. Artificial systems can arrive at the same answers through strategies that diverge from those used by humans, including reliance on spurious shortcuts, non-robust features, or memorized mappings. This motivates examining whether process-level behavioral features provide discriminative information beyond task performance and outputs alone. Because such signatures reflect latent constraints and strategies governing how decisions are generated, rather than merely whether a task is solved successfully, they may remain informative even when human and machine outputs converge. We call this framework a Process Turing Test.

To leverage process traces for distinguishing humans and agents, we lean on insights and tools from cognitive psychology, a field that has long used structured behavioral tasks to infer underlying cognitive mechanisms from measurable regularities in behavior. Examples include working memory limitations, systematic trial-to-trial adaptation following errors, and individual differences in sensitivity to wins and losses. These patterns provide candidate process-level features that can distinguish humans from agents even when task performance, that is, accuracy, is matched. Furthermore, cognitive psychology provides a wealth of tasks validated over decades of research, with established task-dependent cognitive processes we can leverage to explore differences between humans and agents.

However, identifying informative process-level signatures is only part of the challenge. For such signals to serve as robust discriminators, they must remain difficult for agents to reproduce even under targeted adversarial mimicry. Modern AI models are highly adaptable, and can be optimized through fine-tuning or reinforcement learning towards target behavioral objectives, including alignment with human preferences and response styles. This raises a deeper question: even if process-level features distinguish humans from agents, how robust does this distinction remain after targeted adaptation?

We structure our findings in two stages.

First, how much additional discriminative value does process provide beyond output alone? To evaluate this, we introduce a battery of thirty cognitive tasks drawn from cognitive psychology with extensively operationalized process-level behavioral signatures. These tasks were selected to leverage well-characterized differences in how humans solve problems, not merely whether they solve them, thereby providing a rich testbed for comparing human and agent behavior. Across the battery, we find that process-level behavioral features provide substantially greater discriminative signal than performance metrics alone, such as accuracy or earned points. This reveals systematic human-agent differences even when task performance is similar. A random forest classifier reaches an AUC of zero point eight eight across performance-matched tasks.

Second, what limits an agent's ability to reproduce human-like process? We compare frontier agents, Claude Sonnet four point five, GPT-5, and Gemini two point five Pro, with Centaur, a seventy-billion-parameter model fine-tuned on ten point seven million human decisions across more than one hundred sixty tasks, as an approximate upper bound on human-like behavior among current models.

We then progressively fine-tune Qwen two point five Instruct, at one point five, seven, fourteen, and thirty-two billion parameters, and Llama three point three Instruct at seventy billion. We use task-specific imitation of individual human actions, called action-level supervised fine-tuning or A-SFT, and direct task-level optimization of behavioral process features, called process-level fine-tuning or P-SFT.

We find that A-SFT substantially improves human-like process relative to off-the-shelf frontier agents, reaching a mean process distance of zero point four zero, comparable to Centaur at zero point four four. When task-specific process representations are known and aligned to the evaluation task, P-SFT further reduces this distance to zero point two two. However, this advantage largely disappears under cross-task transfer, where P-SFT and A-SFT reach mean distances of zero point six six and zero point six two, respectively.

Together, our results show that process equivalence is independent of output equivalence, and we identify generalization beyond explicit process representations as a central challenge for scalable human-process alignment.

Output versus Process Equivalence

We constructed a battery of thirty tasks largely drawn from cognitive psychology. The battery includes tasks such as mental rotation and the Stroop task, classical psychology tasks with extensively operationalized and validated process measures, as illustrated in Figure one.

We begin by describing the shared human-agent evaluation setup, then use the classical CAPTCHA as an intuitive real-world example motivating the broader benchmark, followed by the full-battery evaluation of agents and humans.

Participants and agents

We administered the thirty-task battery to one hundred human participants recruited via Prolific. All participants provided IRB-approved consent, and ninety-seven were retained after excluding runs affected by platform issues. We also collected fifty full-battery runs from each of three frontier vision-language agents, GPT-5, Gemini two point five Pro, and Claude Sonnet four point five, for one hundred fifty total agent runs.

Agents interacted with the same browser-based interfaces shown to human participants. On each trial, the agent received a screenshot and returned a structured JSON action, for example target coordinates, a tile index, or a categorical choice. A Playwright-based execution layer translated this into the corresponding DOM event, such as a click, keypress, or input. Example task instructions for both agents and participants are provided in an appendix, which is skipped in this reading.

CAPTCHA as a motivating example

We begin with CAPTCHA, a real-world human-machine discrimination task. Modern AI systems can solve many CAPTCHA variants at near-human accuracy. However, matching CAPTCHA performance does not imply matching the behavioral process by which the challenge is solved.

To illustrate this, we compare humans and agents on two CAPTCHA variants. One is a Classic format, in which participants select target images from a grid. The other is a Cross-Tile format, in which participants identify all tiles containing a target object within a single image. Humans and agents achieve statistically indistinguishable performance on these tasks, with a t statistic of negative zero point two nine on ninety-eight degrees of freedom and a p value of zero point seven seven, indicating matched performance. Figure one A shows this.

However, their interaction patterns differ substantially. Process-level features such as sequential click patterns and column and row switches reliably distinguish human from agent CAPTCHA behavior. Thus, output-matched agents need not be process-matched.

While CAPTCHA provides an intuitive real-world example of performance and process dissociation, it captures only a narrow and domain-specific slice of human behavior. We therefore extend this analysis across the cognitive psychology corpus to the remaining tasks in our battery, which span a broad range of cognitive domains and permit systematic measurement of process-level human-agent differences.

The cognitive task battery

The remaining tasks in the battery are classical cognitive psychology paradigms spanning working memory, decision-making, perception, planning, and reasoning. We selected these tasks based on three criteria.

First, the tasks span a broad range of cognitive domains with minimal overlap in the processes they measure. Second, all tasks are well-validated paradigms with established literature characterizing their underlying cognitive processes and associated process measures. Canonical sources for each task are given in the paper's task table. Third, the tasks were configured to require at most ten trials and less than one minute to complete, keeping their duration comparable to that of a CAPTCHA.

Process features were drawn per task from the established process measures of each paradigm's literature. Decision-making tasks capture strategy signatures such as win-stay lose-shift behavior, choice stickiness, delay discounting rates, and early versus late exploration, for example in the Iowa Gambling Task and delay discounting.

Working-memory tasks capture capacity and serial-position signatures such as set-size sensitivity, primacy and recency effects, and transposition errors, for example digit span and Corsi. Perceptual tasks capture bias and precision signatures such as side bias, distance-dependent error, and congruency effects, for example line bisection, visual search, and Stroop. Planning and reasoning tasks capture information-gathering and rule-updating signatures such as pre-decision exploration and perseveration, for example the Mouselab-MDP and the Wisconsin Card Sorting Task.

Process features reveal differences

All human and agent runs were transformed into a common feature vector. It comprised a single primary performance output metric, such as accuracy or total score, and multiple validated task-specific process features. These process features were collected across the thirty tasks and extracted using task-specific featurizers that we applied uniformly to humans and agents. Examples of processes and features are shown in Figure one A.

On output metrics, agents achieved statistical parity with humans on thirteen of thirty tasks, using a Mann-Whitney U test with p at or above zero point zero five. The median Cohen's d magnitude was zero point zero eight on matched tasks, versus one point two nine on unmatched tasks, as shown in Figure one B. This defines a subset of tasks where output-based discrimination would fail.

Notably, many of the tasks on which agents underperformed humans involved continuous visual stimuli, for example biological motion, where continuous human figure movement implies direction, and other video-based paradigms. This likely reflects a limitation of the evaluated vision-language agents, which typically operate over discrete screen snapshots instead of continuous visual streams, rather than a fundamental inability to perform such tasks. As multimodal systems gain stronger support for continuous visual input, performance gaps on these tasks will likely diminish. Therefore, we consider inclusion of these tasks to be appropriate.

While agents achieved output parity with humans on thirteen tasks, process-level features reliably distinguished humans from agents regardless of whether their final outputs were matched. A per-task random forest classifier was trained to distinguish humans from agents using process features, with five-fold stratified cross-validation per task, two hundred trees, maximum depth five, and class balancing. It achieved a mean AUC of zero point eight eight across output-matched tasks, as shown in Figure one C. This is comparable to the mean AUC of zero point eight seven on the seventeen output-unmatched tasks.

Repeating the same analysis using output metrics alone yielded substantially lower discriminative performance. The mean AUC was zero point five five on output-matched tasks, zero point eight zero on output-unmatched tasks, and zero point seven seven across all tasks. Together, these results indicate that process-level behavioral features provide substantially stronger discriminative signal than output metrics alone, and remain informative even when human and agent task performance converges.

Closing the process gap

The preceding results establish that process-level behavioral features reliably distinguish humans from current AI agents even when task performance is matched. The natural next question is what limits an agent's ability to reproduce human-like process. In particular, is the observed process gap primarily due to insufficient exposure to human behavioral data, or does closing it require more direct and task-specific forms of supervision?

We first compare frontier models to Centaur, a foundation model of human cognition. We then fine-tune models, Qwen two point five Instruct at one point five, seven, fourteen, and thirty-two billion parameters, and Llama three point three Instruct at seventy billion, selected to match Centaur's model family and scale. We use progressively more targeted task and process supervision.

Centaur

While off-the-shelf language models are not explicitly optimized to reproduce human behavior in structured cognitive tasks, Centaur is a Llama three point one seventy-billion-parameter foundation model trained via supervised fine-tuning to predict human decisions, using over ten million choices from more than one hundred sixty cognitive experiments. Centaur often outperforms domain-specific cognitive models designed to capture human behavior, making it a strong benchmark for evaluating the extent to which broad human-choice imitation can recover human-like cognitive processes.

Centaur operates exclusively on text-based prompts, so we restrict our subsequent analysis to three tasks with a structured trial format that can be readily represented as text. These are the Iowa Gambling Task, the Wisconsin Card Sorting Task, and the Information Sampling Task. Figure two A gives their descriptions and features. This set of three tasks allows us to directly evaluate Centaur alongside frontier AI agents and our fine-tuned models.

Evaluation metrics

We evaluate agents on these tasks using two complementary metrics.

The first is distributional distance. For each model and task, we computed the Cohen's d magnitude between model and human feature distributions for each process feature, and report the mean across features, together with energy distance between joint distributions. Energy results are in the appendix. Lower values indicate closer alignment to human process patterns.

The second is classifier fool rate. We trained a separate random forest classifier, with two hundred trees, maximum depth five, and class balancing, using only features from the three evaluation tasks. The classifier was trained to distinguish ninety-seven humans from eight models: three frontier AI agents and the base models at each of the five model sizes. Held-out runs from these models were reserved for evaluation, while Centaur and all fine-tuned models were excluded from classifier training. We report each model's fool rate, which is the fraction of held-out runs assigned a probability of being human of at least zero point five, along with the distribution of classifier-assigned probabilities.

Centaur matched human process distributions more closely than frontier agents across the three evaluated tasks, as shown in Figure two B. It achieved substantially lower mean distributional distance: Centaur zero point four four, Gemini one point zero seven, GPT one point three six, and Claude one point six five. Likewise, Centaur achieved substantially higher classifier fool rates than all frontier agents. Centaur's fool rate was forty-four percent, with the remaining models at or below twenty percent. Centaur's mean probability of human was zero point four five, with the remaining models at or below zero point two zero. Full task-specific results are reported in Table three. The narrowing of the process gap from frontier agents to Centaur suggests that large-scale training on human choice data can shift model behavior toward human-like cognitive processes.

Supervised fine-tuning

Results in Figure one demonstrate that process-level behavioral features can distinguish humans from agents even when task outputs are matched, and our comparison to Centaur shows that fine-tuning on large-scale human action data can partially narrow this gap. However, these results do not imply that action imitation represents the upper bound of human-like process mimicry. Human-machine discrimination is inherently an adversarial problem, and thus any behavioral feature used to distinguish humans from agents may itself become a target for optimization.

This motivates a stronger test: can an agent close the process gap when given increasingly direct access to human cognitive process supervision? We test this through two fine-tuning interventions, evaluating each stage in the same way we evaluated Centaur and the frontier agents. First, whether the resulting process-feature distributions move closer to the human distribution. Second, whether a process-based classifier can still distinguish the modified agent from humans.

We tested two forms of fine-tuning.

The first is task-specific action-level fine-tuning, A-SFT. We fine-tuned open-source language models spanning different sizes and model families, using LoRA, to predict the human action at each individual decision point. The models were Qwen two point five Instruct at one point five, seven, fourteen, and thirty-two billion parameters, and a Llama three point three Instruct seventy-billion-parameter model, to match Centaur's architecture. This aligns the model to per-decision human action targets but imposes no explicit objective over run-level behavioral patterns. Small per-trial deviations can accumulate across a session, yielding aggregate process features that diverge from the human population. This intervention tests whether task-specific action imitation, rather than the large-scale imitation provided by Centaur, is sufficient to reproduce human-like process.

The second is process-level fine-tuning, P-SFT. The same set of models was fine-tuned with an additional loss term that directly aligned the run-level process-feature distribution with that of the human population.

At each training step, in addition to the standard cross-entropy loss on individual human actions, we ran the model forward through a complete simulated run of the task and computed its expected process feature vector under its own current policy.

The full training objective, Equation one, says this. The total loss equals the standard cross-entropy loss on human actions, plus a weight lambda times a sum over process features. Each term in that sum is the squared difference between the model's feature value and the human mean, divided by the human standard deviation, scaled by a per-feature weight.

Here the cross-entropy term is the standard supervised fine-tuning loss on human action choices. The model's feature value is a differentiable estimate of that run-level process feature under the current policy. The human mean and standard deviation describe that feature across the human population. The per-feature weights allow up-weighting features that converge more slowly. Lambda controls the relative weight of the feature-matching term against the action-level cross-entropy, with lambda equal to one meaning equal contribution. Hyperparameters are given in the appendix.

As an illustrative example, consider P-SFT on the Iowa Gambling Task. Participants choose among four card decks, A through D, over ten trials. The same procedure was also applied to the Wisconsin Card Sorting Task and Information Sampling Task in our broader fine-tuning experiments. The targeted process features are learning slope, stickiness, deck entropy, win-stay rate, lose-shift rate, and good-deck rate.

On each trial, the model produces a probability distribution over the four decks, for example zero point one five for A, zero point one zero for B, zero point three zero for C, and zero point four five for D. These probabilities feed directly into the feature estimates without waiting to see which deck the model actually picks.

For instance, if the model selected deck C on the previous trial, its stickiness estimate for the current trial is simply the probability of C, zero point three zero, which is the probability of repeating the previous choice. Its good-deck-rate estimate is the probability of C plus the probability of D, which is zero point seven five, since decks C and D are the less risky decks.

At the end of the run, per-trial estimates are averaged into run-level features, such as mean stickiness across all trials. To construct the prompt for the next trial, one deck is sampled from this distribution and the corresponding outcome is appended to the context, for example "You chose deck C and won fifty. Balance: five thousand fifty." This sampled action shapes the subsequent prompt but is not differentiated through. Gradients flow only through the probability-based feature estimates described above. The mismatch between the resulting run-level features and human means is then penalized via Equation one.

We also tested whether any similarity yielded by process-level supervision generalizes beyond the specific behavioral representations used during optimization. We withheld additional process features from the optimization objective and reserved them for evaluation only. These left-out features span distinct behavioral dimensions not directly optimized during training, allowing us to assess whether improvements transfer beyond the supervised process representation. Results

On distributional distance, off-the-shelf language models, namely Claude, GPT-5, Gemini, and base Qwen one point five billion, show large deviations from human process signatures, with distances from zero point eight six to one point six five. Centaur, a seventy-billion-parameter model fine-tuned on human choice data, substantially reduces this distance to zero point four four.

Action-supervised fine-tuning, trained to imitate task-specific human action sequences, and process-supervised fine-tuning, trained to match human process-feature distributions, further reduce the gap across five model sizes. A-SFT achieves a mean distance of zero point four zero, comparable to Centaur, whereas P-SFT yields the closest match to human process signatures at zero point two two, as shown in Figure two B. Across individual model sizes, P-SFT outperforms A-SFT at every scale, with the largest difference at one point five billion parameters, zero point zero six versus zero point six two.

Together, these results suggest that explicit process-level supervision can substantially improve human-like behavioral imitation when the relevant process representation is known and aligned with the target task. However, as we examine below, these gains diminish when models are evaluated on tasks with non-overlapping process-feature spaces. The full set of results, including task-specific results, is provided in Table three and Figure four.

The held-out random forest classifier results mirrored the distributional-distance analysis. Off-the-shelf frontier agents were reliably detected, with fool rates at or below twenty percent. Centaur more than doubled the fool rate of the frontier agents, with forty-four percent of sessions classified as human.

Across model sizes, A-SFT achieved a mean fool rate of forty-two percent, ranging from twenty-seven point five to fifty-two point five percent. P-SFT reached sixty-six percent, ranging from forty-five to ninety point five percent, as shown in Figure three B and C. Thus, while both Centaur and A-SFT substantially increased classifier fool rates relative to off-the-shelf agents, P-SFT produced the largest increase, further demonstrating the advantage of direct process-level supervision in producing behavior that is difficult to distinguish from human behavior.

Generalization tests

To test whether the advantage of P-SFT generalizes beyond the process features and tasks used during fine-tuning, we performed two complementary evaluations.

First, in the left-out feature condition, we fine-tuned each model using only a subset of process features, generated task behavior from the fine-tuned model, and evaluated it using a classifier trained on a separate, left-out set of features, as in Figure three A. These left-out features span distinct behavioral dimensions not directly optimized during training, allowing us to assess whether improvements transfer beyond the supervised process representation. Table two shows consistent results across different optimization and holdout feature combinations.

Second, in the cross-task condition, we fine-tuned each model on one task, generated behavioral rollouts on a different task, and evaluated the resulting behavior using the classifier for that task.

In the left-out feature evaluation, P-SFT retained a modest advantage over A-SFT, although both were roughly close to Centaur, whose classifier fool rate on matched left-out features was forty-three percent. P-SFT's fool rate decreased substantially relative to evaluation on the features used during supervision. P-SFT achieved a mean fool rate of thirty-nine percent, ranging from twenty-two to fifty-nine percent across model sizes, compared with thirty-four percent for A-SFT, ranging from thirteen to fifty-two percent.

In the cross-task evaluation, where models were assessed on tasks with process representations different from those used during supervision, the advantage of P-SFT largely disappeared. P-SFT achieved a mean fool rate of twenty percent, ranging from ten to thirty-one percent, compared with seventeen percent for A-SFT, ranging from eleven to twenty-six percent, with both falling below Centaur.

These findings suggest that process-level supervision yields its largest gains when the target process representation is directly available during training. This advantage attenuates when using left-out features within the same task, and it does not readily generalize across tasks with different process representations. Discussion

Large language models increasingly challenge output-based notions of human-machine discrimination. This motivates a shift from evaluating what models produce, the original Turing Test criterion, to how they produce it, what we call a Process Turing Test. Across our cognitive task battery, process-level behavioral features provided substantially stronger discriminative signal than output metrics alone, reliably distinguishing humans from agents even when their task performance was comparable.

We next asked whether this process gap persists as models are increasingly optimized to imitate human behavior. Centaur, a foundation model trained on large-scale human choice data, substantially narrowed the gap relative to frontier agents. Task-specific action imitation across Qwen two point five and Llama three point three produced similar gains, showing that direct imitation of human actions can shift model behavior toward human process signatures.

Explicit process-level supervision narrowed the gap further, producing the closest match to human behavior when the relevant task-specific process representation was directly accessible. However, this advantage diminished for left-out features and largely disappeared under cross-task evaluation, indicating that matching a known process representation does not necessarily produce more general human-like behavior.

Together, these results suggest that process provides more discriminative power than outputs across current AI models, and that broader human-process alignment will require representations that capture transferable structure across tasks rather than task-specific feature matching alone. Such representations could take the form of latent behavioral embeddings, hierarchical behavioral abstractions, or multi-task objectives that capture shared structure across different cognitive domains.

Limitations

Several limitations remain. First, our human data were drawn from a relatively small participant sample from a single pool. We therefore operationalize "human-like" process relative to the empirical behavioral distribution of this sampled population under the chosen feature representation, rather than as a claim about any canonical form of human cognition.

Second, our fine-tuning evaluation is restricted to a limited subset of our original task battery, as we aimed to benchmark the methods against Centaur, which only accepts text data in a formatted trial structure. Accordingly, it remains unclear whether similar process-alignment dynamics will hold for richer interactive environments.

Lastly, process-based discrimination need not depend on a fixed battery of behavioral probes. Automated task generation and iterative task redesign could continuously introduce novel behavioral demands and target dimensions along which human and machine processes remain distinct. This would turn process-based evaluation into a dynamic red-teaming framework, in which increasingly human-like models are evaluated against new process-level challenges rather than a static set of known behavioral signatures.
