Want more great grantmakers in your field? Offer donation matching as a staff benefit.

My reasoning

We need more good grantmakers. Fieldshapers was started in large part to train up more grantmakers, as many foundations, research organizations and high-net-worth individuals would be keen to deploy more money if they had the support of someone who can vet and prioritize grantmaking opportunities. We got both a huge amount of talent applying, more than seven thousand people, and funders interested, more than ten, in this program. The demand for good grantmakers will be far from filled by the sixteen people we are training up.

Grantmaking makes you better at grantmaking. One of the central mantras of Fieldshapers is that it's much easier to become a good grantmaker if you have capital to deploy. You can learn a lot of things from directly donating, vetting and following up that are hard or impossible to learn in the abstract. We give each fellow one million dollars, but that is with the idea of them deploying ten million dollars or more in the future, and the principle is true even at small scales of grantmaking. Someone deciding where to deploy ten thousand dollars thoughtfully is a very useful excuse for them to get good at deploying one hundred thousand dollars or one million dollars thoughtfully. The sorts of questions you ask, the methodology you use and ways of thinking are amazing training for becoming a full-time grantmaker.

My proposed solution

For individuals: do you want to become a grantmaker long term? Get good at giving out and donating money on small scales. Know why you are giving, what the pros and cons are, and consider many opportunities. It will not be as deep as if you were a full-time program officer, of course, but it's still useful data and will make you a more attractive candidate for that type of job.

For organizations: if you want to create more grantmakers for your movement, give your staff some incentives to donate more. Donation matching is a common perk in the for-profit world, and some major foundations go much further. Gates, Ford and MacArthur all match donations, sometimes at more than one to one. It's a great way to incentivize and train up your staff on grantmaking. It's a no-brainer for foundations to do this, particularly for people in non-grantmaking roles. Often your operations, communications or fundraising staff have an interest in grantmaking, which might even be why they joined your foundation, and it's relatively cheap to give them a solid donation match to start giving them practice. To make it training rather than only a perk, you could ask staff to write a short rationale for each gift, or talk it through with someone on your grantmaking team. Even if you are a smaller organization, something like a one to ten thousand dollar donation match can be a real perk and great for your staff's self-development. The only real "cost" is the difference between what that money would have achieved in your budget and what it achieves at the charity your staff picks. If you are funding-limited, that gap can matter. But many organizations are staffing-limited, not funding-limited, and for those the cost is close to zero, so it makes even more sense to offer this as a perk.

What we do

We offer up to one hundred thousand dollars in matching funding. In theory, this means a staff member could cost almost twice their salary, but we are typically not funding-limited, so in practice it just means other, often smaller but equally impactful, organizations get more support. Longer term, we have a strong intuition that people hired by a philanthropic consultancy will often have grantmaking-related roles in the future. We want them to practice with one hundred thousand dollars before they potentially end up managing one million dollars or more in the next job they get.
