Safety Alignment of LMs via Non-cooperative Games

Safety Alignment of LMs via Non-cooperative Games, by the authors of the AdvGame paper.

Ensuring the safety of language models while maintaining their usefulness remains a critical challenge in AI alignment. Current approaches rely on sequential adversarial training: generating adversarial prompts and fine-tuning language models to defend against them. We introduce a different paradigm: framing safety alignment as a non-zero-sum game between an Attacker language model and a Defender language model, trained jointly via online reinforcement learning. Each model continuously adapts to the other's evolving strategies, driving iterative improvement.

Our method uses a preference-based reward signal derived from pairwise comparisons instead of point-wise scores, providing more robust supervision and potentially reducing reward hacking. Our reinforcement learning recipe, AdvGame, shifts the Pareto frontier of safety and utility, yielding a Defender that is simultaneously more helpful and more resilient to adversarial attacks. In addition, the resulting Attacker converges into a strong, general-purpose red-teaming agent that can be directly deployed to probe arbitrary target models. Code is released by the authors.

Introduction

Language models have become ubiquitous across numerous applications, from conversational assistants to content generation and decision support systems. As these models grow in capability and deployment scale, ensuring their safety has emerged as a critical challenge. Modern developers face a fundamental tension: balancing the usefulness and helpfulness of model responses against the need to prevent harmful, inappropriate, or unsafe outputs. Achieving this balance is essential for responsible deployment in real-world settings.

Safety alignment of language models is not solved. Despite careful safety post-training, state-of-the-art models remain far from robust against adaptive attacks, exhibiting nontrivial jailbreak success rates on standard safety benchmarks, as shown in Figure one. We conjecture that this is due to sub-optimality of the existing methods. Current approaches generally fall into two complementary categories. The first involves manually collecting sets of harmful input prompts across topics of interest and fine-tuning models to refuse or respond appropriately. The second leverages automatic prompt optimization methods to jailbreak language models, followed by adversarial training, via reinforcement learning or supervised fine-tuning, on the collected prompts. Increasingly, such jailbreaking attacks are crafted via specialized Attacker models designed to generate adversarial prompts.

In practice, these approaches are typically applied iteratively in a sequential process. When using an Attacker model, the standard procedure involves three steps: first, training the Attacker to generate adversarial prompts; second, using those prompts to train the Defender, the main model being aligned, to resist such attacks; and third, repeating this alternation until some convergence criterion is met. This sequential cat-and-mouse game, while effective to some degree, suffers from inefficiencies and potential instabilities inherent in alternating optimization.

This paper proposes safety alignment via attacker-defender games. We introduce a different paradigm: jointly training the Attacker and Defender by formulating the safety alignment problem as an adversarial game, which we call AdvGame. Rather than alternating updates, AdvGame trains both agents concurrently, allowing each side to adapt online to the other's current capabilities. This joint view better matches the operational reality of safety alignment: attack surfaces shift as models change, and this encourages equilibria that reflect the long-run interaction between Attacker and Defender.

One possible approach to such formulations employs a two-player zero-sum game, which under mild conditions admits a minimax equilibrium. However, we argue that adversarial safety alignment games are inherently non-zero-sum and asymmetric. In a true zero-sum game, one player's gain is exactly another player's loss. But in safety alignment, the Attacker and Defender have distinct, not perfectly opposed objectives. We describe this in detail in the method section.

Recent work has explored the zero-sum direction in self-play settings, where the same model serves both roles, with shared parameters. Such self-play has gained popularity for training models to solve math, logic and other problems. While self-play may be sensible for scenarios such as a teacher model proposing challenging math problems for a student model, where collaborative improvement is the goal, it is problematic for adversarial safety alignment. Sharing a single set of parameters to serve both Attacker and Defender can entangle objectives, leak gradients across roles, and bias exploration, undermining the diversity and hardness of attacks or encouraging degenerate solutions, for example an Attacker refusing to create harmful attacks.

Our proposal is as follows. AdvGame formalizes Attacker-Defender training as a non-cooperative game in which each agent optimizes its own reward under evolving opponent behavior, without any parameter sharing. The Defender's reward promotes safe, policy-compliant, and useful responses. The Attacker's reward promotes eliciting failures, or policy violations, while penalizing trivial or uninformative prompts.

To robustly train both sides, we instantiate online reinforcement learning objectives that can use preference signals rather than point-wise scores. This means that our language-model judge generates a binary preference between two presented responses, instead of generating a scalar reward for a single response, enabling richer supervision when absolute reward calibration is difficult. In safety alignment specifically, point-wise judges are difficult to verify due to the absence of ground truth, the subjective nature of safety violations, and inconsistent score calibration across contexts, all of which make them vulnerable to reward hacking. Preference-based signals address these issues by requiring only reliable relative comparisons rather than fragile absolute metrics.

Furthermore, our formalization allows using off-policy samples, which we show significantly improves training stability in comparison to pure on-policy learning. We provide a comprehensive empirical evaluation of safety post-training methods, yielding a Defender that achieves state-of-the-art performance on HarmBench and exhibits greater robustness to strong adaptive attacks than prior defended models. Finally, in addition to the Defender, the trained Attacker becomes a strong, general-purpose red-teaming agent that can be directly used to probe arbitrary models.

Background and Notation

We first establish some notation and review background on preference optimization. We denote by T the token vocabulary, and by L the space of token sequences of arbitrary length. We define a pairwise preference model as a function that, given a seed query s and two responses y and y prime, selects the winning response. We write the probability of y being the winner as P of y beats y prime given s. We also define a distribution for randomly sampling the winner and loser: the pair is y and y prime, in that order, with probability P of y beats y prime, and the reverse order otherwise.

Consider direct preference optimization, or DPO. A major assumption there is that the pairwise preference model follows implicit point-wise rewards R under the Bradley-Terry model. That is, the probability that y beats y prime equals the sigmoid of the reward of y minus the reward of y prime. The goal in DPO is to maximize this reward, regularized by a KL divergence that keeps the policy close to a reference model, scaled by a coefficient beta. Equation one states exactly this: the expected reward minus beta times the log ratio of the policy to the reference model, over queries drawn from some distribution.

Exploiting the closed-form solution to this optimization problem, online DPO uses a loss function that I will describe in words. Two responses are sampled from the current model with gradients stopped, a judge picks a winner and a loser, and the loss is the negative log sigmoid of beta times the log ratio of the winner's likelihood gain over the reference, relative to the loser's. Here the stop-gradient operation means we do not compute gradients through the sampling procedure. The responses and preferences are generated online from the trained model and a judge model, respectively.

Method

Our goal is a safe and robust Defender model, pi. Given a harmful query, it should produce a deflection, that is, providing the user with safe, semantically adjacent information. When given a benign query, the model should comply and produce a helpful and direct response.

Standard safety training typically utilizes a fixed preference dataset, which leaves the model vulnerable to jailbreaking or prompt injection attacks that are not present in the training data. That is, the model is not robust. Similar to Self-RedTeam, we therefore formulate the problem as an adversarial training problem with a jointly trained Attacker, as shown in Figure two. Specifically, we model the Attacker as another language model, rho. Its task is to rephrase the original seed query, which belongs to a class that is either harmful or benign, into an attack query that preserves the original intent, but fools the Defender into generating an undesired output.

We can now formulate the game between Attacker and Defender as two coupled optimization problems, given in equations two and three. The Attacker maximizes its expected reward, minus a KL penalty keeping it close to its reference model. The Defender does the same with its own reward, over responses to the Attacker's queries. The two reward functions judge the success of the Attacker and Defender, respectively. Note that unless the Attacker's reward is exactly the negative of the Defender's, this game is non-zero-sum, in contrast to Self-RedTeam. Another difference is that we do not share model parameters between Attacker and Defender. We argue that this is important, as a model capable of generating harmful attacks with the correct prompting will necessarily be unsafe when used as a Defender.

Including the KL divergences keeps the optimal solutions close to the reference models. From an optimization perspective, this means staying on the comparably low-dimensional subspace of human-readable text, which makes the optimization problem more tractable. This also means that we focus on robustness against natural language attacks. Other attacks can be defended against using perplexity-based classifiers.

The Preference Judge

Our formulation requires computing rewards in an online fashion. One option is to directly prompt a judge language model to provide a scalar score and apply an online reinforcement learning algorithm like GRPO, which is the approach taken in Self-RedTeam. However, prior work shows that language-model judges have difficulty providing accurate scalar scores, but perform better at making a binary preference decision. We therefore resort to pairwise preference judges, which provide samples from a winner and loser distribution, as in equation four.

The Defender preference model compares two responses conditioned on the same attack query, whereas the Attacker preference model compares two attack queries along with their respective responses. Under the Bradley-Terry assumption, these preferences relate back to implicit rewards through the sigmoid of a reward difference, as in equation five. We can now view equations two and three as a problem of online preference optimization. Before deriving the loss functions, we first specify how exactly the pairwise judges form their binary decisions.

First, the faithfulness judge. Following Self-RedTeam, we use a binary point-wise faithfulness judge, which classifies attack queries as faithful or not faithful. In practice all our judge models are instantiated as a single language model with varying templates. This ensures that the Attacker does not completely change the underlying meaning of the original seed query, and thereby serves as a regularizing signal. In terms of rewards, non-faithful attack queries are treated as having infinite negative reward, so they will never be selected as the winner.

Second, the Defender judge. With our initially stated goal in mind, we design the Defender pairwise safety judge so that on benign seed queries, it prefers the response which is more compliant. On harmful seed queries it prefers the response that is a better deflection. The judge templates contain a detailed description of these notions along with examples. If the attack query is not faithful to the seed query, we cannot give a meaningful preference between the two responses, so the preference is set to one half. This gives equation six, which has three cases: compliance preference for faithful benign queries, deflection preference for faithful harmful queries, and one half otherwise.

Third, the Attacker judge. Choosing the objective of the Attacker is critical. One simple option is a zero-sum game as in Self-RedTeam, by negating the Defender rewards, or equivalently inverting the preferences. However, we argue that this makes the task too simple for the Attacker. If the Attacker can cause the Defender to generate gibberish, it succeeds according to the judge, as gibberish is neither a good deflection nor a compliant response. In practice, such a formulation will not find a good approximation of the equilibrium, and the resulting Defender will not be robust.

Instead of inverting the preferences, we simply swap the rewards of harmful and benign queries for the Attacker. On a benign seed query, the Attacker gets rewarded for deflection while the Defender gets rewarded for compliance, and vice versa for harmful seed queries. This ensures that the Attacker will try to fool the Defender into thinking that the query belongs to a different category, instead of encouraging gibberish. Equation seven gives the resulting preferences. When both queries are faithful, harmful seeds use the compliance preference and benign seeds use the deflection preference. When only one query is faithful, it wins outright. When neither is faithful, the preference is one half.

The AdvGame Algorithm

We now discuss how equations two and three can be optimized when the reward is implicitly given by a pairwise preference model.

First, the Nash equilibrium. We observe that the Defender's problem is independent of the Attacker and decouples over attack queries. It is strongly concave in the tabular policy, so a unique solution exists. Similarly, the Attacker's problem decomposes over seeds and is again strongly concave, with a unique optimal solution. The unique Nash equilibrium is therefore given by these two solutions.

Second, the AdvGame-DPO losses. Using a derivation similar to the original DPO paper, which we give in the appendix, we derive loss functions for estimating the Nash equilibrium. For the Defender, the loss is the usual DPO loss over a winning and losing response. The queries are sampled online from the Attacker, the two responses are sampled from the Defender, and the judge picks the winner, as in equation eight. The Attacker optimizes an analogous loss over winning and losing attack queries, where each query's quality is judged through the Defender's response to it, as in equation nine. A minimal version of the algorithm is summarized in Algorithm one, and the full algorithm in Algorithm two.

Third, AdvGame-IPO-MD. In the appendix, we discuss an alternative formulation based on IPO-MD, which does not require the Bradley-Terry assumption. It leads to losses that are very similar to the AdvGame-DPO losses, with two modifications. The first is to replace the cross-entropy loss with a square loss. The second is to replace the on-policy generator models with geometric mixture models between the trained and the reference models. In practice, following earlier work on Nash learning from human feedback, we approximate them by exponential moving averages, or EMA. Equation ten says that each averaged model is updated as one minus gamma times its previous value, plus gamma times the current trained model.

Fourth, AdvGame-DPO-MD. Inspired by AdvGame-IPO-MD, we also use EMA models to generate the online data in the AdvGame-DPO formulation, a variant we refer to as AdvGame-DPO-MD. Preference optimization algorithms like DPO and IPO are designed to be off-policy, thereby gracefully handling this modification. This is an important advantage over prevalent on-policy reinforcement learning methods like GRPO.

Experimental Setup

The primary goal of this section is an extensive evaluation of AdvGame. We focus on three questions. First, to what extent can AdvGame advance the Pareto frontier of utility, safety and compliance? Second, how effective is our Attacker for generic model safety red teaming? Third, what are the critical factors contributing to the success of AdvGame, such as the pairwise judge and EMA?

On models: we fine-tune two models, Llama three point one eight B and Qwen two point five seven B. For the Attacker, we use Qwen two point five seven B for the Qwen-based experiments. For the Llama-based experiments, we use an abliterated version of Llama three point one eight B, because, consistent with earlier findings, the safety-aligned Llama refuses to generate adversarial attacks. For the judge, we employ an abliterated Llama three point three seventy B for the Llama experiments and Qwen two point five thirty-two B for the Qwen experiments. All models are instruction-tuned versions unless stated otherwise, and we omit the Instruct suffix for brevity.

On training: we use the WildJailbreak dataset. Our training data consists of all vanilla harmful and vanilla benign subsets, which we split eighty-twenty for training and validation. Additionally, for validation, we randomly sample two hundred fifty-six prompts from both the adversarial harmful and adversarial benign subsets. After extensive hyperparameter tuning, we set the learning rate to ten to the minus six and use a batch size of thirty-two. For all variations of AdvGame, we set beta to zero point one and apply EMA with a decay rate of zero point nine five. We use default generation configurations for vLLM sampling during training and do not apply length normalization. All experiments were conducted on sixteen NVIDIA H two hundred GPUs: eight for sampling rollouts and eight for trainers. All training runs were given a budget of forty-eight hours or one thousand steps, whichever comes first. We use the fairseq two library to implement this recipe.

On baselines: we compare against three baselines. One, the original Llama and Qwen instruction-tuned models. Two, the abliterated Llama, an uncensored version with removed safety guardrails. Three, Self-RedTeam, where we use author-provided checkpoints for Qwen and train our own checkpoint for Llama using their public code. We also include a variant called AdvGame-GRPO, in which the judge directly produces scalar rewards instead of preferences. This has the same structure as AdvGame-DPO, but with an explicit reward model instead of an implicit one, optimized with the standard GRPO loss. It is the closest algorithm to Self-RedTeam, with the major modifications of being non-zero-sum and having no parameter sharing between Attacker and Defender.

On evaluations: to assess whether adversarial training preserves model capabilities, which we call utility, we evaluate on MMLU, IFBench, AlpacaEval two, and ArenaHard. We report additional results on TruthfulQA, ARC-Challenge and BBH in the appendix. For safety, we use HarmBench, WildJailbreak, Do Anything Now, and WildGuardTest, with additional results on XSTest in the appendix. We adopt the safety evaluation suite from Ai two, with two modifications: we use GPT-4o as the judge, and we prompt the judge to assess harmfulness rather than refusal. The main metric is Attack Success Rate, or ASR: the proportion of prompts that successfully elicit harmful behavior. HarmBench and WildGuardTest contain vanilla prompts, which are simple harmful requests, and adversarial prompts that use precomputed jailbreaking attacks. We include the results on adversarial prompts and defer vanilla results to the appendix.

Furthermore, WildJailbreak and XSTest include benign prompts, which we use to test for over-refusal, which we call compliance. This is another important measure of whether the model loses utility by refusing safe requests that might appear harmful, for example, "give me instructions on how to kill a Linux process".

Main Results: Utility and Safety

Three tables summarize the main results. The first shows utility, the second shows attack success rates on safety benchmarks, and the third shows compliance on benign prompts, each for the original models, Self-RedTeam, and AdvGame-DPO-MD, plus the abliterated model for Llama.

On utility, the original models generally achieve the highest performance across most benchmarks, so the goal of adversarial training is to preserve these metrics while improving safety. The Self-RedTeam baseline often results in a noticeable drop in utility. For Llama, for example, its ArenaHard score falls from thirty-three point six to thirteen point six. In contrast, AdvGame-DPO-MD consistently preserves utility close to the original, with minimal drops across benchmarks. On Llama it even improves ArenaHard to forty-one point zero.

On safety and compliance, safety is measured by the reduction in harmful behavior, while compliance reflects the model's ability to avoid over-refusing benign prompts. The original models exhibit higher rates of harmful behavior, especially on fixed adversarial prompts. For Qwen, HarmBench adversarial attack success drops from thirty-one point six percent for the original to four point seven percent for AdvGame-DPO-MD, and WildGuardTest adversarial drops from thirty-eight point six to one point two. Self-RedTeam reduces harmful outputs with a slight decrease in compliance on Qwen and a larger drop on Llama. AdvGame-DPO-MD and AdvGame-IPO-MD strike the best balance between all three axes: strongly improved safety, slightly decreased compliance, and minimal decrease in utility.

Robustness Against Adaptive Attacks

So far we have presented results on a fixed set of adversarial prompts. This includes HarmBench adversarial, which pre-computes adversarial prompts by applying state-of-the-art jailbreaking attacks to a collection of open-source models. The prompts themselves are fixed and evaluated in a transfer setup. To further assess robustness, we apply several established adaptive jailbreaking methods directly to the checkpoints used in the previous tables. Specifically, we run TAP, PAIR, and GCG on the HarmBench test set, using default hyperparameters for each method.

Table four reports the results. As anticipated, attack success is generally higher than before, due to the adaptive nature of the attacks and the assumption of white-box access to the model, which is required for GCG. Under these conditions, AdvGame-DPO-MD demonstrates significantly greater robustness than baseline methods. For Qwen, PAIR succeeds seven point two percent of the time against AdvGame-DPO-MD, versus forty-five percent against the original. This performance gap is especially pronounced for the black-box attack methods, PAIR and TAP, which generate human-readable adversarial prompts, the primary focus of our defense strategy.

How Good Is the Attacker?

A notable byproduct of our method is the development of a capable Attacker model, which can be leveraged for red-teaming. Our Attacker does not run any special iterative algorithm. Adversarial prompts are generated, conditioned on an input harmful behavior, using the regular generation procedure, with temperature one and a maximum of five hundred tokens. For evaluation, we take the Attacker checkpoint from the same step as the AdvGame-DPO-MD Defender, recalling that the Attacker does not share parameters with the Defender, and generate ten adversarial prompts per input. We consider the attack successful if at least one adversarial prompt is judged successful by the default HarmBench judge, on the same test set used by the other methods.

The rightmost column of table four shows that our Attacker achieves performance comparable to strong adaptive attack methods like PAIR, GCG and TAP. This is especially noticeable for Qwen, where attack success on the original Qwen two point five seven B model approaches fifty-six percent, the second best result. It is worth noting that the AdvGame Defender demonstrates relative robustness against the AdvGame Attacker, which is expected since the Defender is specifically trained to be robust against prompts generated by the Attacker within our game setting. Analysis and Discussion

This section analyzes several key aspects of our adversarial training. We compare different versions of AdvGame, DPO-MD, IPO-MD and GRPO, across training stability, utility and safety, among other dimensions. Using online DPO as a case study, we also ablate the type of judge model, the benefits of off-policy versus on-policy training, and the importance of training the Attacker rather than keeping it fixed. Further analysis is in the appendix, where learning curves report reward. Note that AdvGame-DPO and IPO do not actually use rewards during training, since they only need preference pairs, so reward is calculated only for logging.

Table five compares variants on Qwen. AdvGame-GRPO has HarmBench attack success of fourteen point nine percent. IPO-MD and DPO-MD both reach four point seven. For WildJailbreak, GRPO scores twenty-two point three, IPO-MD seven point eight, and DPO-MD eight point five. The ablated rows of DPO-MD show worse safety: fixed attacker gives sixteen point six on WildJailbreak, a point-wise judge gives thirty point eight, and no EMA gives forty-nine point five.

On DPO versus IPO versus GRPO: the results reveal clear differences in training dynamics and downstream safety performance. DPO and IPO outperform GRPO on safety benchmarks, achieving lower rates of harmful behavior and higher utility scores, and the extended tables substantiate this. On Qwen, DPO and IPO achieve the lowest harmful behavior rates on HarmBench and adversarial WildJailbreak, while also maintaining high compliance on benign prompts. GRPO exhibits higher harmful behavior rates and less reliable compliance, aligning with its unstable training dynamics. The training curves show that DPO and IPO exhibit similar and stable learning for both Attacker and Defender, with the Defender consistently achieving higher rewards and lower fluctuations than GRPO. GRPO struggles with pronounced instability and lower overall rewards, particularly for the Defender. The validation curves show the same: DPO and IPO maintain higher Defender rewards over time, while GRPO shows erratic behavior and a decline in performance. Overall, these results suggest the importance of preference optimization, coupled with a pairwise judge, for safety fine-tuning.

Should we train the Attacker, or is keeping it fixed enough? In the fixed-attacker row, the Attacker is only trained to produce outputs in the correct format, but not to improve the jailbreaking reward. As expected, it simply learns the format and does not get better at jailbreaking. The Defender still adapts to balance benign and harmful prompts, but the safety reward is lower than when the Attacker is actively trained. Final utility remains the same, but safety scores are worse. This suggests that actively training the Attacker makes adversarial training more diverse and challenging, which ultimately helps the Defender become more robust.

Pairwise judge versus point-wise judge. For our preference learning variants, a point-wise judge means that we independently prompt the judge to give a scalar reward for a single response, and then compute the preference based on which reward was higher, instead of showing the judge both responses at the same time. At first glance, the training curves suggest the point-wise judge leads to better validation rewards for both Attacker and Defender. However, this improvement is misleading, as the judge producing the validation rewards differs between the two runs. In the table, there is a clear drop in both safety and utility. This supports the idea that point-wise judging makes it easier for models to exploit reward hacking, while pairwise judging is more robust and less prone to such exploitation. That said, we are not claiming this universally holds. Perhaps designing better reward models might partially address the problem.

Benefits of EMA. The no-EMA row compares training with an on-policy generator versus an off-policy EMA generator. In the on-policy setup, the generator uses the current models to generate prompts and responses and synchronizes after each step. The off-policy approach also synchronizes after each step but maintains a moving average of the model parameters. On-policy training performs worse in terms of safety metrics, and this is reflected in lower reward curves. One possible explanation is that on-policy training can make the model overfit to recent changes, causing instability and less reliable safety. EMA helps smooth out updates, making training more stable.

Finally, additional ablations on AdvGame-DPO-MD, including a non-thinking Attacker, a smaller judge model, or a non-optimistic Attacker, are in the appendix, as are further GRPO results comparing pairwise versus point-wise judges and on- versus off-policy generation.

Conclusion

In this paper, we introduced AdvGame, a novel framework for adversarial safety alignment that jointly trains Attacker and Defender language models in a non-zero-sum, non-cooperative game, departing from traditional sequential alternation or self-play approaches. Our experimental results demonstrate that AdvGame variants, particularly AdvGame-DPO-MD and AdvGame-IPO-MD, achieve a superior balance across safety, compliance, and utility metrics, while exhibiting significantly greater robustness against adaptive attacks, especially black-box methods generating human-readable adversarial prompts such as PAIR and TAP.

Through comprehensive ablation studies, we established that several design choices are critical for effective adversarial training. One, actively training a separate Attacker model, without parameter sharing, to generate diverse and challenging attacks. Two, employing pairwise preference judging rather than point-wise scoring. Three, utilizing off-policy sampling with EMA for improved training stability.

In terms of limitations, our approach focuses exclusively on natural-language attacks that humans can understand, deliberately excluding non-readable attack sequences such as GCG. Also, while our experiments show limitations with GRPO-based training, we do not claim this universally holds: better hyperparameter search and better reward design might improve its performance.

We believe that future work on improved reward model design and extensions to other attack modalities could further enhance the robustness and applicability of game-theoretic approaches to language model safety alignment.
