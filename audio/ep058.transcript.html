AI's Future Is Still a Choice.

The Future of Work

AI is rapidly changing how some companies work, but most businesses are struggling to turn AI capabilities into real productivity gains, panelists said during a discussion at Stanford. "I've never seen such a gap between the capabilities and what's actually on the ground," said Erik Brynjolfsson, the Jerry Yang and Akiko Yamazaki Professor and an associate director and senior fellow at Stanford HAI. Meta offered an example of the potential: Andrew Bosworth, Meta's chief technology officer, said a two hundred person team had surpassed, in eight months, work that previously took twelve hundred people four or five years to build. Across other businesses, however, integration problems, accuracy concerns, and limited training are slowing adoption.

Entry-Level Jobs

Of major concern: fewer entry-level jobs and opportunities for skills development when AI handles the tasks workers once learned in those first roles. Brynjolfsson said his research found a significant decline in hiring of early-career workers in highly exposed occupations like coding and customer service. He pointed to entrepreneurship as one opportunity, arguing that AI can help people identify and tackle problems with fewer resources.

Accuracy

Another issue: accuracy. At Snowflake, AI has brought greater demands for data accuracy, security, and governance, said Anahita Tafvizi, Snowflake's chief data and AI officer.

A Matter of Choice

Whether workers share in AI's benefits will depend on choices companies and society make, panelists argued. Bosworth called for continuous education and warned against repeating past failures to retrain displaced workers. Brynjolfsson said he expected AI to generate wealth but worried that its rewards would become increasingly concentrated. "A lot of people aren't going to participate in it, but it's not an inevitability," he said. "It's very much a choice."
