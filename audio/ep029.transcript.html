California's Race-Based Pregnancy Funding — Effort

California's Race-Based Pregnancy Funding, from Effort.

The Department of Justice could cut funding to programs exclusively for "Black Women and Birthing People."

In April twenty twenty-six, a pregnant woman in Pasadena sued the Los Angeles Department of Public Health after they denied her medical services because she was not black.

The complaint says that, after learning that Mrs. Jimenez is not black, a B I H program coordinator informed Mrs. Jimenez that the program was not for her. Mrs. Jimenez was denied publicly funded services for healthy pregnancies and early childhood development, services available to others solely because they fit the government's preferred race.

California birth and motherhood programs continue to deny medical care based on race. Three discriminatory California programs spend millions in federal Medicaid funding annually, making them susceptible to Department of Justice enforcement. They are the CalAIM Enhanced Care Management Birth Equity Population of Focus, called Birth Equity; the Black Infant Health Program; and the Perinatal Equity Initiative.

The programs and the laws

A table lists the civil rights law violations the author alleges for each program. For the CalAIM Birth Equity program, it cites the Fourteenth Amendment's Equal Protection Clause, Title Six of the Civil Rights Act, Section fifteen fifty-seven of the Affordable Care Act, and related federal rules barring race discrimination in federally assisted programs and in all operations of a state or local health agency. It quotes the state's Birth Equity guidance saying that managed care plans are expected to partner closely with organizations serving Black, American Indian and Alaska Native, and Pacific Islander individuals.

For the Black Infant Health Program, the table quotes the state health department: "We Serve: Black women and birthing people who are sixteen years or older, pregnant, or up to six months postpartum at the time of enrollment, regardless of income."

For the Perinatal Equity Initiative, it quotes the department describing a community-driven initiative designed to address racial disparities in infant mortality by providing meaningful support and resources to Black families.

The money

All three programs receive federal Medicaid funding and matching funds from the State of California. Based on state records, Effort estimates that Birth Equity bills thirty-two point one seven million dollars per year, including an estimated sixteen point zero eight million dollars in federal Medicaid funding. That estimate applies a public contracted rate of four hundred dollars per enrolled member per month to six thousand seven hundred two publicly countable enrolled members in the third quarter of twenty twenty-five. The fifty percent federal rate used is a conservative base-rate scenario, since the public enrollment data omit the eligibility codes needed to compute the exact share.

A chart of sources shows the funding figures, drawn from the state's award notice, its funding page, and county agreements.

The state health department lists annual spending of twenty-four point eight million dollars for the Black Infant Health Program and seven point two million dollars for the Perinatal Equity Initiative. The Black Infant Health spending includes three point nine million dollars in federal Title Five block grants, in addition to Medicaid. In Fresno County, Black Infant Health receives five hundred thirty-five thousand nine hundred ninety dollars in federal Medicaid reimbursements, thirty-seven point two percent of its budget, and the Perinatal Equity Initiative receives one hundred twenty-seven thousand one hundred eighty dollars, seventeen point six percent of its budget. Because not all counties provided this breakdown, the statewide percentages are estimates.

The law and enforcement

The author says the authors of the Civil Rights Act and its expansions promised that it granted equal protection against anti-white discrimination. Section six hundred one of the Civil Rights Act of nineteen sixty-four states: "No person in the United States shall, on the ground of race, color, or national origin, be excluded from participation in, be denied the benefits of, or be subjected to discrimination under any program or activity receiving Federal financial assistance."

However, the prohibition on anti-white discrimination was rarely enforced in practice. That changed in twenty twenty-five, when the Department of Justice issued new policy guidance. On July thirtieth, twenty twenty-five, the Department said that federal antidiscrimination laws apply to programs or initiatives that involve discriminatory practices, including those labeled as Diversity, Equity, and Inclusion programs. Attorney General guidance of July twenty-ninth added that federal funding recipients may also be liable if they knowingly fund the unlawful practices of contractors, grantees, and other third parties.

In twenty twenty-six, the Civil Rights Division began to act on anti-white discrimination. It closed a Minnesota Department of Health review after the state ended a grant favoring organizations "serving and led by people of color." It also closed an Illinois review after the state removed a thirty percent racial set-aside from a loan-repayment program.

If the State of California does not remove racial requirements from its programs, Title Six or Section fifteen fifty-seven review could suspend their federal Medicaid funding.

As this investigation shows, California's racial preference system is not going down without a fight.
