Right to Buy split my estate. Now its days could be numbered

Right to Buy split my estate. Now its days could be numbered.

Growing up in a council flat in the nineteen eighties and nineties, my mum would often point out homes that tenants had bought from the council. You could usually tell just from looking. A plain wooden door had been replaced with something fancier. New windows had appeared. Sometimes there was a porch or pillars outside the front door.

Those houses were bought under the Right to Buy scheme, arguably the most consequential housing policy of my lifetime. It was introduced in nineteen eighty, three years before I was born, when Margaret Thatcher's government gave council tenants in England the legal right to purchase their homes at a discounted price.

Supporters welcomed the chance for working-class families to own their own home and build wealth. Critics argue it hollowed out the country's stock of social housing and contributed to today's housing crisis.

Some of my neighbours "made a killing" from the policy, in the words of my mum. One bought an almost identical flat to ours in nineteen eighty-nine for twenty-one thousand two hundred pounds, after receiving a discount of eighteen thousand eight hundred pounds. Eight years later it sold for fifty-four thousand five hundred pounds. Five years after that it changed hands again for one hundred and forty-three thousand pounds. Notably, the person who made the most money from that flat wasn't the original council tenant. It was the people who bought it afterwards.

But Andy Burnham doesn't like the policy. The Right to Buy scheme has seen various changes since its introduction in the nineteen eighties, and Labour has weakened it significantly since coming to power in twenty twenty-four. Then, in September, the prime minister told Labour's conference: "We will reform Right to Buy. Not taking the right away from anyone in an existing home, but removing it from new homes." This would help to keep council houses in public ownership "for generations to come", Burnham told delegates.

He has previously compared trying to solve the housing crisis while continuing to sell social homes to "trying to fill a bath with the plug out".

More than forty years after its introduction, Right to Buy remains a debate about political values as much as housing policy, balancing the home ownership ideal against the desire to preserve social housing for future generations.

A property-owning democracy

Since nineteen eighty, more than two million homes have been sold through the scheme in England.

In fact, about forty percent of England's council housing stock has been sold through Right to Buy. Most of those sales took place in the early years, says Professor Rebecca Tunstall, a housing academic at the University of York and the London School of Economics. At the time, Labour was vehemently opposed to Thatcher's plans. Yet many of its own voters went on to benefit from them.

Labour now argues that not enough of those two million homes were replaced. Sir Keir Starmer's government restricted the scheme, including by heavily reducing the maximum discount that councils were allowed to give tenants.

And now Burnham is removing the right to buy entirely from any new-build social home. The measures are included in a bill currently making its way through Parliament. The government says that before Labour's various restrictions on the scheme, England was projected to see a net loss of twenty-six thousand council homes over the next decade. Now there will be a net gain of eighteen thousand homes over the same period, it calculates.

This opens up a political divide: some opposition parties, like the Conservatives and Reform UK, say they would keep or even extend the scheme.

For those who became homeowners, Right to Buy turned a dream into reality.

Barbara Stevens, now eighty, bought her two-bedroom council flat in Kentish Town, north London, through Right to Buy for around thirty-five thousand pounds. Now mortgage-free, she estimates it could be worth between six hundred thousand and seven hundred thousand pounds.

"I've always wanted to own my own property," she says.

Her friend Trevor Abrahmsohn believes her story demonstrates the success of the scheme. "If you want to condemn the poor to poverty, make them lifetime renters," he says.

Those arguments were central to Thatcher's vision of a "property-owning democracy".

Few people are more closely associated with Right to Buy than Lord Heseltine, who was the cabinet minister responsible for housing when it was introduced. When he and Thatcher launched the scheme in nineteen eighty, council tenants were offered substantial discounts to purchase their homes.

A relative of mine whose parents bought their council home in Croydon told me it was the only time "anyone would ever give you a hundred grand for free".

Even Housing Secretary Angela Rayner, part of the government that's restricting Right to Buy, bought and later sold her own council home for a profit.

In nineteen eighty, Lord Heseltine described the scheme as the biggest transfer of capital wealth from the state to ordinary people. "It certainly was," he says now, almost half a century on.

I ask him if, looking back, he would have designed the policy differently. "No," he answers.

He thinks Right to Buy gave its beneficiaries an important asset. "People who bought their own homes made a great deal of capital gain," he says.

A double charge for taxpayers?

But to its detractors, Right to Buy is responsible for many of Britain's housing woes.

Professor Tunstall says the policy is partly responsible for making social housing scarcer, which meant more people competed for homes in the private rented sector, pushing up rents.

Lord Heseltine says it was originally assumed that councils would use sale proceeds to support new housing. He acknowledges that did not happen after he left government.

That failure to replace sold homes sits at the heart of the modern criticism of Right to Buy.

For Molly Harris from the New Economics Foundation, it is "private landlords who are the real winners".

Her think tank's research found that forty-one percent of homes sold through Right to Buy are now privately rented out. "Homes that were built with public money, and with the aim of providing an affordable and secure path to home ownership, have since been turned into assets," Harris says.

She says the shortage of social housing means more people need help with housing costs in the private rented sector, where rents are higher. So taxpayers are effectively paying twice: once to give a discount on the initial home; and then again, via housing benefit, when that home is rented out to someone who can't afford the payments. A former council home rented privately could now be valued at double, triple or quadruple what it cost when it was under local authority control.

I put that criticism to Lord Heseltine, asking him whether the scheme has failed because some ex-council homes are now effectively being rented back to the state, at higher costs.

He dismisses it as "rubbish", adding: "The scheme was wildly successful in spreading ownership across the country."

According to Harris's research, the government spent more than fifty-eight billion pounds between twenty twenty-one and twenty twenty-five "subsidising private landlords" through the benefit system.

Newham Council says almost half of the nine thousand council homes sold under Right to Buy in Newham were later "flipped" into private rentals. The borough now has the biggest council house waiting list in the country. Last year, The Big Issue reported that Newham Council made a loss of more than twenty million pounds of taxpayers' money buying back homes it had sold just a few years earlier. The council said it was a quick way to provide new housing, and was a cheaper option than some alternatives.

And some council tenants say they are not interested in buying their homes. Ruth Crabb has lived in her flat in Havering since nineteen ninety-seven. "I wouldn't buy my flat because I didn't want it to go out of circulation, as social housing is for those who come after me," she says. But, she adds: "I don't judge anyone who bought their property with Right to Buy."

The ownership premium

Supporters of Right to Buy also argue that you can't appreciate the true value of the scheme by looking at numbers on a spreadsheet.

The benefits of the policy extended for generations, they say, helping the children and grandchildren of those who bought homes.

Research co-authored by Matteo Sandi, an economics professor at the University of Milan, looked at children growing up in areas where Right to Buy uptake was highest. He found better educational and economic outcomes, including higher GCSE attainment, greater chances of attending university, and increased earnings in adulthood.

"What we find is that there is, in fact, a lasting benefit from growing up in an owned property," he says.

He believes it's because ownership encouraged families to invest more heavily in their homes and neighbourhoods.

One of my relatives backs this up. He tells me his parents were able to move elsewhere in the country, enjoy a better standard of living and finally afford things that had previously been out of reach. He believes Right to Buy transformed their lives.

But Professor Tunstall argues home ownership is not the only route to a stable family life. "The critical things for safety and for children's development and for happy families are security and affordability," she says, and children can also get that from social renting, she thinks.

Looking back, that resonates.

When I was a child and my mum rented from the council, she knew what she would be charged every month. She knew we wouldn't suddenly be forced to move. My siblings and I knew we wouldn't have to change schools. At that point, in the nineteen eighties, one third of houses in England were council-owned. When my mum became pregnant with me, it was safe to assume that a home would soon become available, and within about a year, one did. She moved out of my nan's and into her new council flat.

The children I now meet through my work often do not have that certainty. Today, the average wait for a family-sized home in London is twenty-seven years. Some children are moved repeatedly between hotels and hostels. Councils across England now spend eight million pounds every day housing families in temporary accommodation.

Sandi is careful not to present his findings as an unconditional endorsement of Right to Buy.

"My take is the Right to Buy scheme was a good policy with a big limitation," he says. "More social housing should have been built."

Limits of reform

But curbing Right to Buy could take a long time to bear fruit, says Professor Tunstall. Scotland abolished its Right to Buy programme in twenty sixteen and Wales followed in twenty nineteen, but Professor Tunstall says it "hasn't made a huge difference" yet in those nations, because the changes are too recent. Northern Ireland continues to operate a scheme similar to Right to Buy.

And deeper challenges will remain; Right to Buy is not the only culprit of Britain's social housing shortage, she says. Supply has also been reduced by council estate-clearance programmes, she says, which saw thousands of homes demolished from the nineteen nineties. Like Lord Heseltine, she also blames decades of under-building: had governments continued to build at their nineteen eighties rates, she argues, the sale of council homes might not have resulted in an overall loss of social housing.

The housing landscape has also changed dramatically since nineteen eighty. Many councils transferred responsibility over council housing to housing associations, meaning they have lost the skills and expertise to build their own stock.

More than twenty-five years later, I return to the street where I grew up. It looks much the same, although a few new front doors suggest more homes have been sold. My mum never took advantage of Right to Buy; she continues to rent a council flat on a small estate in south London.

I ask her if she regrets that. She says she simply wouldn't have been able to afford it.

I tell her that the flat she could have bought for around twenty thousand pounds would now be worth somewhere between three hundred and fifty thousand and four hundred thousand pounds.

"You're kidding?," she replies.

No-one in the nineteen eighties could have known what would happen to house prices or the cost of living.

But whatever people think about Right to Buy, there is broad agreement on one point: the problem was not necessarily selling the homes. It was failing to replace them.
