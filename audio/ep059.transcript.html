Feature translation

The feature translation is titled: The final struggles of a batch of near-death robotics companies.

Context: Xu Chuan, a pseudonym, still holds out hope. His former employer, a Guangdong-based robotics startup, owes him six months of salary, yet he has not pursued legal action. "The boss is actually a decent person," he reasons. Plus, he hopes the company can recover, choosing to ignore the fact that it has consistently failed to find stable commercial footing despite several pivots.

Similar stories have unfolded throughout China's robotics industry this year. In July, Yinghe Robotics, once valued at five hundred million U.S. dollars, completed bankruptcy procedures. Two months earlier, DeepBlue Robotics, a company backed by the AI unicorn DeepBlueAI, also filed for bankruptcy.

The winner of last week's Around the Horn was a longform Huxiu article by Yuqin Yi, packed with interviews and data. It details why so many Chinese robotics firms are barely hanging on to life. It ties together themes from two recent ChinAI issues. First, the defiance of common sense in China's venture capital ecosystem when it comes to embodied AI, from ChinAI number three eight one. Second, China's unsustainable embodied AI sector, built on murky guanxi relationships and limited technical capabilities, from ChinAI number three seven two.

Key takeaways

Key takeaways: DeepBlue Robotics and Yinghe Robotics are prominent failures, but they are part of a broader trend.

From the article: "An industry insider familiar with DeepBlue recalled that the company expanded rapidly over the past few years, extending its operations from smart retail into areas like autonomous driving and robotics. Its robotics division alone spanned various categories, including cleaning, delivery, disinfection, and gardening. 'Basically, whatever was hot, they would do,' the source noted."

After the breathless reports of initial deals, what actually transpired when it came to project fulfillment? In the case of DeepBlue, nothing. For instance, in twenty twenty-one, the company boasted that South Korea's LG Group had agreed to procure tens of thousands of intelligent disinfection robots. But: "A former DeepBlue employee stated that they were never executed on a large scale, attributing this to the products' inability to adapt effectively to real-world operational environments."

Again, it's not just these high-profile crashouts. Huxiu compiled data on nearly fifty robotics manufacturers that have faced operational crises or exited the market over the past five years. The article presents this in a table, which lists those companies.

Many Chinese robotics applications have not matched their technology to actual demand. One interviewee shared an illuminating story with Huxiu about his former employer, a firm that specialized in robotics for cleaning building facades. You would think this would be a perfect product-market fit, given that high-altitude cleaning is so risky for humans. However, according to this source: "'The technology wasn't up to the task, but they managed to fudge it,' he recalled his former employer addressing the issue. When the robots failed to complete a job, human workers would step in to finish the task, and then the human labor costs were folded into the project budget." The Chinese word for fudge here is húnòng.

Industrial settings demand higher levels of cost-effectiveness and stability than humanoid robots can offer. Per Huxiu's statistics, "among the two hundred eighteen publicly disclosed projects related to embodied intelligence and humanoid robots in China during the first half of twenty twenty-six, approximately fifty-five point five percent were procured by educational and research institutions."

It's possible that financial backers are waking up to these cold hard realities, which will only compound problems faced by robotics startups just trying to hang on. Yi reports, "Entering the second half of twenty twenty-six, financing activity in China's domestic embodied AI sector began to cool. In the third quarter, total funding fell to thirty-three point three billion RMB, a decline of roughly twenty-nine percent compared to the quarterly average from the first half of the year."

To close, Jeff says he should give some weight to the alternative interpretation. As the piece cites, IDC data shows a growing market for some applications, namely lawnmower and pool-cleaning robots. One could argue that these failures are part of a natural weeding-out process in a competitive industry. Still, coverage of China's robotics and embodied AI sector needs to be rebalanced toward the struggles and "fudges," and away from press releases disguised as reporting.
