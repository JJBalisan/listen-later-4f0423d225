America's Covid Fraud Totals Over Three Hundred Billion Dollars, from Effort.

We counted likely fraud in all state and national programs, including executive actions.

The US government spent four point five trillion dollars on pandemic relief through March thirty-first, twenty twenty-five. Federal inspectors general flagged over three hundred billion dollars of that money as likely fraudulent. The staggering degree of Covid fraud is downstream of policy choices, particularly around unemployment.

The Small Business Administration's inspector general identified over two hundred billion dollars of Paycheck Protection Program and Economic Injury Disaster Loan loans for investigation on June twenty-seventh, twenty twenty-three: one hundred thirty-six billion dollars in Economic Injury Disaster Loans, and sixty-four billion in the Paycheck Protection Program. Meanwhile, the Government Accountability Office estimated that one hundred to one hundred thirty-five billion dollars of the regular and temporary unemployment benefits paid between April twenty twenty and May twenty twenty-three went to fraudulent claims, for a total of three hundred to three hundred thirty-five billion dollars.

Why verification failed

Trillions of dollars in Covid benefits required almost no verification. Benefits were paid based on the theory that employees lost job opportunities due to government policy. Often, nothing was done to verify these claims with employers or past financial records before paying out.

The Small Business Administration was the center for Covid fraud. It distributed one point two trillion dollars in Covid benefits through the Paycheck Protection Program and COVID-19 Economic Injury Disaster Loan loans and advances. Unemployment programs paid about eight hundred eighty-eight billion dollars, the Employee Retention Credit refunded two hundred eighty-three billion through June twenty twenty-five, and Lost Wages Assistance, created by presidential memorandum in August twenty twenty, paid thirty-six point five billion.

Other federal estimates

The Labor Department's inspector general estimated one hundred ninety-one billion dollars in improper payments, based on its typical twenty-one point five two percent improper-payment rate. Given the lower scrutiny during Covid, this is likely an underestimate.

The department uses six high-risk criteria for this estimate, including claims filed in several states under one identity, Social Security numbers belonging to the dead or the imprisoned, and implausible ages. These cover forty-six point nine billion dollars of claims paid between March twenty twenty and April twenty twenty-two. The Pandemic Response Accountability Committee found seventy-nine point four billion dollars across the Paycheck Protection Program, Economic Injury Disaster Loans and unemployment, paid on applications whose Social Security numbers failed a match against Social Security Administration records.

As of February twenty twenty-six, the IRS had not estimated improper payments in the Employee Retention Credit. Its criminal investigators had opened five hundred forty-five cases covering five point six billion dollars in claims by February twenty twenty-five.

The state role

Unemployment benefits are distributed through states, meaning that transparency and fraud detection are bottlenecked by state reporting. State unemployment fraud is rarely investigated; of every dollar flagged, under two cents have been established, and of every dollar established, eleven cents have been recovered.

Covid fraud overwhelmingly took advantage of federal funding. The Paycheck Protection Program, Economic Injury Disaster Loans, the temporary unemployment programs, Lost Wages Assistance and the federal grants that states administered were funded with federal money. Regular unemployment insurance uses state trust funds, while California's Golden State Stimulus used state revenue.

State agencies established five point four eight billion dollars in fraud across Federal Pandemic Unemployment Compensation, Pandemic Unemployment Assistance, Pandemic Emergency Unemployment Compensation and Mixed Earner Unemployment Compensation. They recovered five hundred ninety-five million dollars, or eleven percent.

Because politically interested states control what information is reported to federal authorities, reporting methods and reliability vary wildly across states. California's auditor identified more than thirty billion dollars in potentially fraudulent claims across two fiscal years. New York's comptroller extrapolated eleven billion from a sample, while the state's labor department projected four billion on one hundred five billion paid. Michigan's eight point four to eight point five billion comes from a Deloitte model that counts eligibility errors alongside fraud, and Arizona's four point three billion is identity theft the agency itself identified.

Incentives

Legislators defined political victory by the amount of funding paid out, not by the rate of fraud or efficacy of payments. They were not incentivized to prevent fraud. This leads to opposite dynamics in private versus public fraud prevention. In the private sector, auditors and investors constantly evolve to defend against fraud. The government rubber stamps approvals, setting static standards which are easily bypassed, if they exist at all.

Covid programs funded at the state level are led by California and New York. Six programs in California and New York paid out thirteen point seven three seven billion dollars of state money. California's auditor found more than sixty million dollars in erroneous Golden State Stimulus Two payments out of about six billion distributed. New York's comptroller found four point one million dollars in ineligible small-business grants against seven hundred sixty million awarded.

Recovery

It can take years to recover fraudulent payments, if they are returned at all. On September twenty-third, twenty twenty-six, the Small Business Administration referred one hundred billion dollars in potentially fraudulent Paycheck Protection Program and Economic Injury Disaster Loan loans to the IRS for tax investigation. It also reported roughly one million suspended borrowers tied to forty-nine billion dollars in suspected fraud, along with twenty-two billion dollars in outstanding balances sent to the Treasury for collection. Less than one percent of that was recovered by the end of fiscal year twenty twenty-five.

The Justice Department's False Claims Act settlements and judgments over pandemic programs totaled eight hundred twenty million dollars. The Feeding Our Future prosecution in Minnesota established two hundred forty million dollars obtained on pandemic child-nutrition claims, and its ringleader was sentenced to five hundred months in May twenty twenty-six.
