Fiona the hippo may have been unique, but she was not uniquely deserving of our empathy.

There's a good chance that many of you will have already heard about Fiona the hippo and her recent death. The story of her life and her tragic end is a story that teaches us about the individuality and strength of animals, while at the same time providing us with necessary and important context around our relationship with them.

Fiona's life and death

Fiona the hippo was born prematurely in Cincinnati Zoo in twenty seventeen. While a normal birth weight for a hippo is between twenty-five and fifty-four kilograms, Fiona only weighed thirteen kilograms when she was born. Her chances of survival were viewed as being extremely slim, and the zoo even brought in staff from Cincinnati Children's Hospital to assist with her treatment. Against all odds, Fiona managed to survive and became a healthy adult hippo.

Considering that she was also very cute, it is perhaps unsurprising that people became invested in Fiona's life, her resilience and survival making her a celebrity animal who people wanted to follow. For good and for bad, Fiona became viewed in a way that very few animals are, her life elevating her to a source of pride for Ohio and a source of joy for people who watched videos of her online.

Tragically, Fiona died on the nineteenth of November. Her death occurred because she ingested a hat which had been thrown into the air by a child. The hat caught onto a ceiling fan and was thrown into the enclosure through a gap at the top of the glass partition. A spokesperson for the zoo said: "We probably couldn't make that happen again if we tried. It was clearly an accident."

The online reaction

While the rest of this article will be scrutinising the ethical inconsistencies that most of the people mourning Fiona's death will have, I do also want to acknowledge that the genuine upset that people have felt about her death is a testament to the fact that we as humans can, and should, foster deeper levels of empathy and compassion for non-human animals.

All that being said, looking into the reaction of people online has been quite overwhelming. The sheer volume of responses and the levels of irrationality have been difficult to process. In a previous article, I discussed the killing of HeeHaw, a pet donkey who was shot and killed by a police officer. In that story there was someone who could be blamed. This time it's less obvious where the finger of blame should lie.

This has led to a huge amount of anger and fury being directed at the child and their parents, with people actively trying to find out who they are. At the time of writing, the sequence of events is still unknown beyond what the zoo has said about the hat being thrown into the fan and accidentally ending up in the hippo enclosure.

If you look online, though, people claim they know the age of the child, that the child was misbehaving, that the parents are bad parents, that they left without telling any of the staff the hat had blown in, and the list goes on. These details might be true, they also might not be, but these details all create the narrative that there is someone who can be blamed, in this case a child, who many people believe should be punished.

Who is to blame

I do find it quite astonishing the level of vitriol and hate being thrown at a child, whose age is still unknown, by people who pay to have animals killed. A lot of the online discourse feels more akin to a pitchfork mob mentality than it does genuine sorrow about Fiona's death. People call vegans militant and extreme for saying that it's immoral to pay for animals to be forced into gas chambers, meanwhile a child has accidentally killed an animal and people are calling for the child and their family to have their lives effectively ruined.

If there is any blame, it would seem that most of it should be levelled at the zoo itself. Why was there a gap at the top of the enclosure? If an object as insignificant as a hat can kill the animals, there shouldn't be gaps that can allow for hats or other objects to end up in the enclosures. The purpose of this article is not to discuss in detail the ethical issues with zoos, but the author does have a video about zoos, which was linked in the original.

Maybe the parents should and could have stopped the child throwing a hat. Maybe the child was twelve years old and was messing about when they shouldn't have been. Then again, perhaps all the people claiming the parents need to go to jail and the child should be publicly shamed and made to suffer need to take a look in the mirror. "People who live in glass houses" comes to mind.

Moral inconsistency

For example, you can buy a double cheeseburger with bacon at the zoo. Not only is animal agriculture the leading cause of habitat and biodiversity loss, issues that zoos are supposed to care about, it is also the cause of hundreds of billions of animals suffering and being killed. Rather than the reaction to Fiona's death being reflective of a broader care for animals, Fiona is the moral anomaly for most people: an exception to the rule that our actions condone animals needlessly suffering and being killed.

Fiona had an incredibly difficult start to her life, overcoming adversity and becoming a symbol of strength and hope. But what about the farmed animals lucky enough to be rescued? What about the animals who jumped out of trucks taking them to slaughterhouses or who escaped from the kill floor seconds before they were about to be slaughtered? Why are they not seen as symbols of strength and hope? What could show more strength than overcoming your oppressor and defiantly liberating yourself from systemic violence?

The animals we eat are born into a world where almost every single person on the planet wants them to be killed. Fiona was born into a world where almost every single person who knew of her wanted her to live. All Fiona ever experienced was care and concern, while billions of animals alive in farms at this very moment will not even experience collectively the empathy and compassion that Fiona received.

I don't make these comparisons to take anything away from Fiona but instead to emphasise that animals are victims of our moral myopia. It's not that the animals we eat aren't complex, feeling beings like Fiona, it's that we refuse to acknowledge them as being such. We position Fiona as being special, as being uniquely deserving of our empathy, while ignoring that every single year incomprehensibly large numbers of animals, who are just as deserving of the same empathy, are needlessly and brutally killed. These animals aren't killed because of an accident, they have their lives deliberately and intentionally taken from them, forced into houses of slaughter that exist with one purpose: to stab, cut, gas and bleed them to death.

Farmed animals

Whenever cases like this happen and we as vegans attempt to shine a light on people's ethical inconsistencies, the argument that we are often met with is that the animals we eat are "farmed animals." Because of this, the animals we eat are different and are not like other animals who do deserve compassion. The animals we eat should be viewed as lesser because they were bred to be killed and eaten.

We don't apply this standard consistently. We don't believe that dogs who are bred to fight are less deserving of empathy than animals who are not bred to fight. We don't even apply this standard consistently with the animals that we do eat, with many people who eat cattle still being against bullfighting, regardless of whether the bulls were bred with the intention of being used for fighting.

The American Hippo Bill

Before the First World War, a bill was introduced to the United States House of Representatives known as the "American Hippo Bill." The bill proposed importing hippos to Louisiana with the intention of killing and eating them. President Theodore Roosevelt and the United States Department of Agriculture reportedly backed the plan, and the New York Times referred to hippo meat positively as "lake cow bacon." While the bill didn't end up being passed, it's still interesting to contemplate what could have been if hippos, like cattle, had been imported to the United States and viewed as a "food animal."

Events like this highlight how arbitrary our view of animals can be, our perception of cattle based on legal and cultural norms, not justifiable ethics. If things had gone differently in nineteen ten, the way hippos are viewed in the US could have ended up radically different. However, this difference in perception would have had nothing to do with hippos themselves. Instead, it would simply be to do with the legal and cultural norms that could have ended up being different if hippos had been brought to the US and viewed as "food animals."

Conservation and fame

People also can't claim that Fiona is different because she came from an endangered species, as Nile hippos are neither listed as vulnerable nor endangered. Even if they were, the idea that zoos release animals into the wild is one of the many misconceptions that exist around them. If people care about wildlife conservation, rather than buying a ticket to see an animal in an enclosure, they would do far more good by donating that money to a conservation organisation that is actually working with animals in the wild.

Fiona was internet famous, her personality and struggles shared widely online and garnering a significant following. Her internet fame meant that her life was perceived as being more valuable and important than the lives of just about any other animal on the planet. This is not morally or logically true, not because Fiona's life wasn't important or morally valuable, but because the lives of other animals matter too. Fiona may have been unique, but she was not uniquely deserving of our empathy.

Closing

What happened to Fiona the Hippo was awful and shouldn't have happened.

What happens to cows, pigs, chickens, lambs and fish is awful and shouldn't happen.

If you only agree with one of those statements, then perhaps now is a good opportunity to reflect on why some animals are considered deserving of compassion while billions of others aren't.
