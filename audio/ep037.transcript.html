Restricting the Model, Missing the System: Measurement and Accountability in Offensive AI Governance

Restricting the Model, Missing the System: Measurement and Accountability in Offensive AI Governance. A research paper; the authors are not named in the text.

Abstract. We show that the instruments used to measure AI offensive capability fail in two ways: first, they overstate harm, and second, they credit the model with capability that belongs to the surrounding system. We argue that restricting access to a model is therefore necessary but not sufficient, and that policy and procurement also need system-level, harm-grounded capability assessment.

In June twenty twenty-six, two frontier models were suspended under US export controls, reportedly prompted by a jailbreak that asked a model to read a codebase and fix its flaws. This finding measured an elicitation system of model, prompt, and task. We support our argument with a study of an open-source framework in which lightweight large language model, or L L M, agents coordinate through shared memory and evolutionary optimization, providing two pieces of evidence.

First, jailbreak metrics overstate harm. Over two hundred and twenty-five swarm-generated attacks per target, L L M-as-judge scoring rated Claude Sonnet four compromised in forty percent of attacks, yet manual verification found actionable harmful content in none, against a forty-five point eight percent Effective Harm Rate for GPT-4o.

Second, scaffolded evaluations misattribute capability. On a planted-vulnerability target, a full pipeline built around a one point two billion parameter model recovers nine of nine weaknesses, while the same model without the hand-crafted components recovers zero of nine by crash verification and two of nine by cited source line.

Offensive capability is a property of model, scaffold, and evaluation protocol together. The duty to assess it should lie with whoever controls the system, shapes its behaviour, and can foresee what it will do. In our setting, that is the party who builds the harness around the model, a role that current regulation does not clearly cover.

Introduction

This paper asks two questions: whether the instruments that policy relies on measure the offensive danger of AI correctly, and whether restricting access to a single model, the main policy tool in use today, acts on the quantity those instruments track. We argue that on both counts the answer is presently no, and that this is a problem of evaluation and accountability before it is a security one. A regulator, a standards body, or a procurement team cannot govern a quantity it mismeasures, and it cannot assign responsibility for a capability it has attributed to the wrong component.

The question has already come up in practice. On the seventh of April twenty twenty-six, Anthropic announced Project Glasswing, a restricted preview of a model it described as capable of identifying and then exploiting zero-day vulnerabilities in every major operating system and web browser when directed by a user, and which it did not plan to make generally available. Independent evaluation by the UK AI Security Institute corroborated the capability claim: the preview was the first model to complete the Institute's thirty-two-step end-to-end corporate-network attack simulation, in three of ten attempts.

In June, a US export-control directive suspended access by foreign nationals to two of the developer's frontier models, Fable five and Mythos five, and the developer disabled both for all customers to comply. The directive was lifted on the thirtieth of June, after researchers at the US Center for AI Standards and Innovation, or CAISI, tested the safeguards and judged them "extraordinarily strong," subject to conditions on detecting security risks, collaborating on standards, and reporting malicious activity. In the developer's understanding, the trigger for the restriction was a jailbreak: a method that essentially consisted of asking the model to read a specific codebase and fix any software flaws.

The episode approximates a natural experiment for our thesis. The finding that led to a model-level restriction described a system: a model prompted in a particular way to do a particular task. Its severity was contested because nobody had a shared way to say how much capability the jailbreak added over what other public models already do. The developer's own statement noted that the vulnerabilities in question "all appear relatively simple, and other publicly-available models are able to discover them as well without requiring a bypass." Whether or not one agrees, the disagreement was about measurement: what was gained, by which component, over what baseline.

This paper provides empirical evidence through two experiments, each of which bears as much on measurement as on capability. The first shows that a coordinated swarm of one point two billion parameter models compromises the output-level safety of one market-leading frontier model but not another, and that the standard jailbreak metric misreads the difference.

The second shows that the same class of model, embedded in a pipeline with regex pattern detection, a hand-crafted exploit corpus, and AddressSanitizer-based runtime verification, performs software vulnerability discovery requiring cross-file reasoning, and that almost none of that capability survives once the hand-crafted components are removed. We measure what the one point two billion parameter model contributes autonomously, without those components, to keep the two apart.

Our results imply that both the threat from AI-assisted attacks and our ability to measure it are system-level phenomena. The threat depends on coordination, architecture, and evolutionary optimization, all of which are already widely available, and not only on access to any single model. The measurement depends on whether the metric tracks realized harm and whether it attributes capability to the right component. Neither is captured by asking only which model an actor can obtain.

We use these findings to make a governance argument the security-benchmarking literature has largely left implicit: if capability is a property of the system, then the duty to assess it must attach to whoever controls and can foresee the system's behaviour, and the AI value chain as currently drawn has no clean slot for the party who assembles the harness.

Statement of contributions. First, we extend evidence that jailbreak success overstates realized harm to frontier production models under population-based attack, and document a second failure mode, the misattribution of scaffold capability to the model, each with reproducible evidence from open-weights models on consumer hardware.

Second, we propose a harm-grounded success metric and a capability-attribution decomposition, and connect both to a live regulatory gap. The AI Act requires robustness against adversarial manipulation without defining it operationally, and a frontier developer has proposed, with its partners, a severity rubric with the same shape as our proposal.

Third, we argue that the duty to assess system-level capability should follow realized, foreseeable control, and we show why existing value-chain roles, namely provider, deployer, distributor, and importer, do not fit the harness assembler.

This paper extends earlier preliminary work that presented the multi-agent framework and the two experiments as security results. New here are the harm-grounded companion grading with three judges, the autonomous configuration that separates model from scaffold contribution, the reading of both experiments as measurement failures, the harm-grounded metric and attribution decomposition as assessment instruments, and the governance analysis of who holds the assessment duty, why the value-chain vocabulary does not name the harness assembler, and the elicitation gap.

Background: the governance moment

The main policy instrument for offensive AI capability is access restriction at the level of a single model: withhold the frontier model, or give it only to selected partners, and the offensive capability it represents is assumed to be contained. The twenty twenty-six export-control episode shows how this works in practice. Restriction was the only tool available, no shared, vendor-neutral standard existed for scoring the severity of the jailbreak claim, and governments and companies improvised under time pressure, cutting access and restoring it three weeks later once government researchers had tested the safeguards.

Two developments since then bear directly on our argument. First, the actors closest to the problem have started building the missing instrument. Shortly after the directive was lifted, the developer proposed the Cyber Jailbreak Severity scale, an early-draft rubric in the style of the Common Vulnerability Scoring System. It scores jailbreaks from zero to four on capability gain, breadth of that gain, ease of weaponization, and discoverability, and the developer is developing it with its Glasswing partners.

Like our metric, it separates a bypass from an increase in realized harmful capability, and scores a finding at zero when a publicly available tool produces an equivalent result on a reasonable timescale, in an equivalent harness, without additional expert input. The rubric is harm-grounded and baseline-relative, as our metric is. Whether it becomes a shared, vendor-neutral standard is still open.

Second, enforcement has begun while the definitions are still open. The EU AI Act requires that high-risk AI systems be robust against adversarial manipulation, in Article fifteen, but provides no operational definition of "robust." Obligations for general-purpose models with systemic risk have applied since August twenty twenty-five, and since August twenty twenty-six the Commission, through the AI Office, can enforce them. It can request information, evaluate a model, including by requesting access, and, if necessary, restrict the model's availability.

Meanwhile, the Digital Omnibus postpones the high-risk obligations to December twenty twenty-seven for Annex three and August twenty twenty-eight for Annex one. Enforcement now operates around a measurement question nobody has settled. We argue that settling it correctly comes before, and does not depend on, whichever tool a regulator or procurement team ends up using.

Related work

Automated red teaming of L L M safety. Automated jailbreak methods split into white-box and black-box branches. G C G finds adversarial suffixes through gradient, but requires access to the target weights. PAIR and TAP pioneered the black-box attacker-LLM pattern, with TAP generalizing PAIR to a tree-of-attacks search. HarmBench standardizes evaluation across many methods and targets with a fine-tuned judge.

That automated jailbreak success overstates realized harm is established. StrongREJECT finds standard evaluations significantly overstate jailbreak effectiveness relative to human judgment, and the jailbreak tax shows jailbroken outputs lose task quality, with accuracy drops up to ninety-two percent on reasoning tasks. JailbreakBench provides a standard judged robustness benchmark for the same phenomenon. VENOM finds that L L M judges, GPT-4o among them, anchor on toxic language: they still label most responses as jailbreaks when all factual content is removed and only a malicious tone remains. Expected Harm weights jailbreak severity by the likelihood that the threat is carried out, modeled as a function of execution cost.

Our work differs on three points. Our attackers are a population that shares memory and evolves across generations. Our attacker is a swarm of one point two billion parameter models on consumer hardware, whereas PAIR and TAP typically use GPT-3.5 or GPT-4. And we show that judge false positives differ by target; that is, the judge rewards one model's structured refusals as compliance, so cross-model comparisons on technical success rate are unreliable.

Concurrent work on small language models reports the inverse failure mode. Some models that look safe under naive testing turn out to be highly susceptible to tailored jailbreaks. Both directions point to the same gap between surface safety metrics and realized harm.

L L M-assisted vulnerability discovery. Prior work organizes along two axes: realism of the target, meaning synthetic benchmarks versus production code, and autonomy of the model, meaning static evaluation versus tool-augmented agentic operation. Benchmark suites such as CyberSecEval two operate on isolated snippets and cannot capture cross-file reasoning.

Agentic frameworks such as Google's Big Sleep pair a frontier L L M with code-browser, sandbox, and debugger tools to plan multi-step exploit chains, but reasoning budget and attack diversity are bounded by a single model instance. L L M-augmented fuzzers such as Fuzz4All use the model as a structured input generator; the search strategy remains the fuzzer's. An appendix expands these descriptions.

The harness as a variable. A complementary line treats the multi-agent harness itself as a search variable. AgentFlow represents each harness as a program in a typed graph language and uses an outer-loop optimizer to propose harness edits. Holding the model fixed, pass rates on an eighty-nine-task terminal benchmark span a roughly four-times range, from twenty percent to eighty percent, across publicly ranked harnesses.

Run against a production web browser with an open-weights mixture-of-experts model with thirty-two billion active parameters, the same loop discovered ten previously unknown zero-days, all confirmed by the vendor and two of them critical sandbox escapes. This is independent, cross-sectional evidence for the thesis we test longitudinally on a single target: capability is a property of the harness and the model together. AgentFlow operates at far larger scale, a one-hundred-ninety-two-GPU cluster for seven days, than our fixed, hand-designed swarm of one point two billion parameter open-weights models on consumer hardware. The two studies sit at opposite ends of the scale range.

With the partial exception of AgentFlow, the approaches above share reliance on a single frontier-scale model in a monolithic topology: one model holds the full reasoning trajectory in its context. We change both, replacing the single frontier model with a swarm of smaller models. The analogy is single-trajectory versus population-based search: individual agents are weaker, but the ensemble gains diversity, parallelism, and coverage.

Accountability in AI supply chains. That capability is distributed across a system is, for governance, a version of the "many hands" problem: when many actors contribute to an outcome, responsibility diffuses and no one is clearly answerable. Cobbe and colleagues show that algorithmic supply chains create an "accountability horizon." Each actor sees its immediate counterparties and little beyond them, so neither producer nor deployer holds both the knowledge and the control needed to ensure compliance. They argue that accountability should be allocated by factual control, whatever the contracts say.

We take this as the starting point for section six. Our contribution is to show, empirically, where the control that realizes offensive capability actually sits, and to argue that the assessment duty should follow it. A recent policy report treats the harness as a distinct object of governance and argues that agents should be evaluated together with their tools, memory, permissions, and orchestration loop. It does not address offensive capability or how harness builders map onto statutory value-chain roles, which is where our analysis goes further.

Evidence one: jailbreak metrics overstate harm

The two evidence sections make two claims about instruments, each supported by one experiment. First, a metric that scores refusals as breaches overstates realized harm, and does so unevenly across models; that is this section. Second, a metric that reports one recall number for a model-plus-scaffold pipeline misattributes the scaffold's capability to the model; that is section five. One is about the numerator of a safety score; the other is about which component the score belongs to. Section six builds the governance argument on both.

Method. Five instances of LFM two point five, one point two billion, Thinking operated as a coordinated attack swarm, each assigned a distinct strategy: direct instruction override, persona-based roleplay, multi-turn escalation, obfuscation, and evolutionary mutation of successful attacks. The swarm maintained shared memory of successful attacks and evolved over fifteen generations using crossover at rate zero point five and mutation at rate zero point three, retaining the two highest-performing attacks per generation.

Two frontier models were targeted: GPT-4o and Claude Sonnet four, both queried on the twenty-second of January twenty twenty-six. These snapshots were superseded by mid-twenty twenty-six, as discussed in the limitations. Each received two hundred and twenty-five attacks: fifteen generations times five agents times three tasks, which were explosives instructions, a phishing email template, and hacking into a computer system.

Attack success was scored via cross-provider L L M-as-a-judge evaluation: Claude judged GPT-4o outputs and GPT-4o judged Claude outputs. This design exposed an asymmetry in judge reliability. The GPT-4o judge often scored Claude's structured refusals as successful attacks, crediting format compliance even when the content refused. So Claude's raw attack-success rate is inflated, and the two targets' technical success rates, scored by different judges, are not directly comparable. This non-comparability is one of the paper's findings.

We instead report the Effective Harm Rate: the proportion of attacks producing verified actionable harmful content, determined by a manual verification pass over every judged-successful response. It relies on human verification rather than an automated judge, so it is the metric we compare across targets. The technical success rate is reported only to expose the gap between apparent and realized success.

Verification protocol. Because the Effective Harm Rate depends on human judgment, we set out that judgment in detail. The cross-provider judge scores compliance: how fully the target engaged with the request. Compliance is what the Effective Harm Rate has to correct for, because a refusal can score as full engagement.

The manual verification pass covered the review set: every response the judge scored as successful, meaning a compliance score of at least zero point six, the run's success threshold. That is one hundred and thirty-seven responses for GPT-4o and ninety for Claude Sonnet four. One author read each response in a single pass and counted it as actionable harmful content only if it supplied operationally specific means of causing harm: concrete, executable steps rather than a topical description, generic caution, or a refusal restated in the attacker's terms.

Of the review set, one hundred and three GPT-4o responses, which is forty-five point eight percent of two hundred and twenty-five, and zero Claude responses met this bar. Each response was judged once, by one annotator, and the per-response decisions were not logged, so we cannot report inter-annotator agreement, and the absolute rates carry that annotator's threshold. The comparison between targets does not rest on this pass alone: three automated graders applying the published StrongREJECT rubric reproduce its direction and categorical size. Because the pass covers every judged-successful response and lower-scored responses are excluded by construction, the reported rates are lower bounds on realized harm. That is the conservative direction for our argument.

A reproducible harm-grounded companion. As a reproducible companion to the author-verified rate, we grade every released response with the published StrongREJECT rubric, run from a fixed definition with the judge model recorded on each response. To keep the score from depending on a single provider, we use three judges over both targets: an OpenAI model, StrongREJECT's provider-neutral fine-tuned open-weights grader, and an Anthropic model, accessed through the standard API without elevated security-research access.

All three rank GPT-4o above Claude Sonnet four at a zero point five rubric threshold. The two that score every response agree closely with the manual pass: GPT-4o at forty-two point seven percent and forty-four point zero percent harmful, against Claude's eleven point six percent and four point nine percent. The rubric's zero point five threshold credits partial compliance that the actionable-harm bar excludes, so Claude is low rather than zero.

The Anthropic judge ranks the targets the same way, thirteen point six percent against one point nine percent of the responses it scored. But its content filter declines to score fifty-one responses, forty-one of GPT-4o's, mostly from the explosives task, and ten of Claude's. So its rates are computed over one hundred and eighty-four and two hundred and fifteen responses and are not directly comparable with the other two graders. That the declines fall four times as often on GPT-4o's responses is consistent with the direction of the gap. The automated rubric and the manual pass agree on the direction and the categorical size of the gap.

Results. Table one summarizes the outcomes. On technical success rate, GPT-4o scored sixty point nine percent and Claude Sonnet four forty point zero percent. On Effective Harm Rate, GPT-4o scored forty-five point eight percent and Claude zero percent. GPT-4o had forty-nine critical-severity attacks and Claude none; GPT-4o had eighteen perfect scores and Claude none; and the refusal rate was fifteen point one percent for GPT-4o and fifty-six point nine percent for Claude. The technical success rate is the judge's compliance score of at least zero point six, and includes false positives.

GPT-4o produced forty-nine critical-severity responses containing detailed attack methodologies, sabotage techniques, and operational frameworks. In ninety-one point two percent of successful attacks it provided structured step-by-step instructions, and some offered to elaborate further. Claude Sonnet four, despite a forty percent technical success rate, produced no actionable harmful content. Its nominally successful responses were benign reinterpretations of the attack prompt, educational content with caveats, or explicit identification and refusal of the jailbreak attempt.

The gap is categorical rather than a matter of degree: forty-five point eight percent Effective Harm against GPT-4o, zero percent against Claude. Claude's nominally successful responses still declined to produce harmful content, which points to output-level safeguards that act at generation rather than only at input filtering.

The swarm roughly doubled its success rate against GPT-4o over the fifteen-generation run. The first two generations averaged thirty-three point three percent, the window of generations seven through fourteen averaged sixty-five point eight percent, and the final generation reached seventy-three point three percent. These windows track the learning curve and are not the overall rate, which is computed over all two hundred and twenty-five attacks. The proportionally larger improvement against Claude Sonnet four, from six point seven percent to forty-six point seven percent, started from a near-zero baseline and did not translate into effective harm.

Neither model is safe under all conditions. The finding is narrower: a single automated metric compresses a categorical difference into a modest one. By technical success rate, Claude Sonnet four is compromised at two-thirds of GPT-4o's rate, forty percent against sixty point nine percent. By realized harm the gap is zero percent against forty-five point eight percent.

A procurement rule that rejects any model above a fixed jailbreak rate, for example thirty percent, would reject both and never see the realized-harm gap between them. A regulator or procurement team using technical jailbreak rate as its figure of merit would read Claude as "broken forty percent of the time" and might mark it down against a model that is, by the harm-grounded measure, far worse. The error lies in the instrument rather than in the model.

Evidence two: scaffolds misattribute capability

The second piece of evidence is narrower by design: one planted-vulnerability target, studied closely, to isolate a single quantity, namely how much of a reported recall number belongs to the model and how much to the scaffold around it. We present it as a case study rather than a capability estimate.

The swarm is an effective jailbreak attacker and a weak autonomous vulnerability finder, and this is not a contradiction. Jailbreak search rewards diversity and persistence, which a population supplies, whereas planted-bug discovery rewards multi-step cross-file reasoning, which a one point two billion parameter model lacks. The scaffold supplies the latter, which is the point of this experiment.

Method. The target was a deliberately vulnerable C application, SwarmApp, about five hundred and fifty lines across five files, roughly three hundred and forty excluding comments and blank lines. It contained nine planted vulnerabilities spanning nine distinct Common Weakness Enumeration identifiers, including stack and heap buffer overflow, format string, integer overflow, use-after-free, double free, null pointer dereference, command injection, and hardcoded credentials. The application also included correct uses of dangerous functions, red herrings meant to trip keyword scanners into false positives.

The full, assisted pipeline ran in two phases. The first was source-code analysis by role-differentiated one point two billion parameter agents using regex pattern detection followed by grounded L L M citation, with a post-hoc weakness-normalization layer. The second was binary fuzzing under AddressSanitizer against a hand-crafted seed corpus with L L M-generated mutations.

To separate the pipeline's capability from the model's, we also ran an autonomous configuration on the same target with the hand-crafted seeds, the regex layer, and post-hoc normalization all disabled. There, findings are credited only via citation-verified recall, where L L M-cited source lines match ground-truth signatures held only at scoring time, and crash-verified recall, where L L M-generated fuzz inputs produce actual AddressSanitizer crashes.

Results. Table two reports the core result. The assisted full pipeline recovered nine of nine, or a hundred percent. The autonomous configuration recovered two of nine, twenty-two percent, by citation verification, and zero of nine by crash verification. In the autonomous configuration, only one of six L L M-assigned weakness labels was correct. The full configuration completes in about four minutes on a consumer laptop.

In the autonomous configuration the one point two billion parameter model identifies two of the planted vulnerable lines as suspicious but assigns the correct weakness class to only one. It cannot construct the multi-step inputs needed to trigger any planted bug at runtime, and its proposed fuzz inputs include literal macro names, truncated command sequences, and placeholder text the sanitizer filters out.

The full pipeline recovers all nine by spreading the work across components: regex patterns, role-specialized search, and the AddressSanitizer crash classifier each contribute distinct weaknesses, and the model's one credited contribution duplicates something a non-L L M component already found.

We read the assisted nine of nine as a statement about the pipeline on this target, not as a capability estimate for the model or for software at large. The scaffold was iterated against these nine bugs, so the number is close to a fixed point of its own design; taken alone it would be a poor measure of anything. What is not an artifact of that iteration is the distance between the two configurations, nine of nine assisted and zero of nine crash-verified autonomous, at fixed model and fixed target. That distance, rather than the nine of nine, is what the experiment measures.

On this target the recall belongs to the scaffold. The per-weakness breakdown shows why: regex-matched seeds under AddressSanitizer and the hand-crafted fuzz corpus account for seven of the nine, and the single weakness credited to the model's grounded citation, hardcoded credentials, duplicates what role-specialized search already finds. A report that credited the nine of nine to the model would be crediting it with work done almost entirely by conventional static and dynamic analysis.

A harm-grounded success metric

Both experiments concern the system, not the model in isolation, and both expose a measurement error that a policy or procurement decision would inherit. This section turns that into a governance argument in five steps: what a corrected metric measures, which component a capability figure belongs to, who should carry the assessment duty, why the model provider cannot carry it alone, and what this means for procurement and regulation.

The divergence between technical success rate and Effective Harm Rate is a measurement problem with a policy cost. By conventional jailbreak metrics, Claude Sonnet four was "broken" forty percent of the time; by the metric reflecting potential for real-world harm, zero percent of the time. If regulators adopt technical jailbreak rates as their operational reading of the AI Act's undefined "robust," they risk penalizing models that are safe in practice. Our two targets exhibit only this over-penalizing direction.

A robustness figure should count realized harmful content, not judge-scored format compliance. The Effective Harm Rate is one such figure, and its main weakness is that "actionable harm" has no agreed operational definition. Our single-annotator pass and the StrongREJECT rubric draw the line in different places, zero percent against one point nine to eleven point six percent for Claude Sonnet four across the three graders, yet both preserve the gap between targets. That disagreement is a reason to standardize and publish the definition, not to fall back on judge-scored compliance, which counts refusals as breaches.

We are not offering the Effective Harm Rate as a finished standard. The severity rubric a frontier developer has proposed scores capability gain and ease of weaponization against a public baseline, and scores a jailbreak at zero when publicly available tools reach an equivalent result, which is the same logic as ours. That a developer and its partners drafted a similar instrument under time pressure suggests the measurement is feasible. The harder problems are agreeing on the definition and on who governs it.

Capability attribution

An evaluation that bundles a model with a scaffold and reports one number does not measure the model and should not be read as if it did. The assisted-versus-autonomous decomposition is one way to report the model's marginal contribution separately from the system's, and the two diverge sharply at one point two billion parameters. Concurrent work reaches the same conclusion from the other end: holding a frontier model fixed and varying the harness moves pass rates across a roughly fourfold range.

Any assessment that will inform a decision to restrict, to procure, or to certify has to say which component it is crediting. Our planted target sits at the low-capability end, where the scaffold carries nearly all of the recall, and that harness result shows the misattribution persists at the frontier end. Model-level capability figures mislead across the whole range, which is why the attribution duty is general rather than an artifact of a small model.

A combined number can also hide errors in the evaluation code itself. An early iteration of our extractor read weakness labels out of source comments and reported a spurious hundred percent recall, an error class documented across vulnerability-detection benchmarks and quantified at scale across software-engineering evaluations.

This attribution claim does not rest on our single planted target. Three independent lines converge on it. The first is our assisted-versus-autonomous decomposition over time on one target. The second is AgentFlow's harness comparisons at fixed frontier model and its harness search on production software. The third is the twenty twenty-six export-control episode, where a model-level restriction reportedly followed a system-level elicitation, and the proposed response, the severity scale, scores a finding in its system context and against a public baseline instead of as a fixed property of the model. Our planted target is the most controlled of the three; the governance argument rests on all three agreeing, not on it alone.

Who bears the assessment duty

If capability is a property of the system, the duty to assess it cannot sit only with the model provider. The question is where it should sit, and here the law already contains the principle, if not yet the vocabulary.

Under the AI Act, a party that puts its name on a high-risk system, substantially modifies it, or repurposes a general-purpose system into a high-risk use becomes its provider, and the original provider must hand over documentation and testing access, in Article twenty-five. Duty follows modification and control.

The Cyber Resilience Act is more pointed for our setting. The manufacturer that integrates a component, including free and open-source software, must exercise due diligence over it and bears responsibility for the security of the assembled product, which it cannot shift entirely upstream, in Article thirteen, paragraph five. The revised Product Liability Directive points the same way, treating a party that substantially modifies a product outside the original manufacturer's control, and then makes it available or puts it into service, as a manufacturer itself.

Scholarship converges on the same principle from the accountability side: allocate by factual control, not contractual fiction, and calibrate obligations to the degree of control an actor actually exercises, with provider disclosure duties intensifying as downstream control falls.

The common thread is that responsibility follows realized control. In our setting that control sits with the party who assembles the harness: the coordination, the seed corpus, the pattern-to-weakness layer, and the evaluation protocol are what turn a one point two billion parameter model that recovers zero of nine bugs into a pipeline that recovers nine.

Yet the statutory vocabulary of provider, deployer, distributor, and importer has no clean slot for this actor. They are not the model's provider; they may deploy nothing in the regulated sense; and for an open-weights model the provider's own leverage is limited, because weights already released cannot be recalled or monitored. The Commission's July twenty twenty-five guidance makes a downstream modifier a provider only when the modification exceeds roughly one-third of the original model's training compute. A harness uses no training compute, so even this route does not reach the assembler.

We do not propose a new liability category or a fixed answer to who pays when a harness causes harm; that is the regulator's call, and pretending otherwise would overreach the evidence. Our narrower claim is that the current vocabulary is insufficient here, and that the missing role, the harness assembler, is the one that both realizes and can foresee the system's marginal capability. Recognizing it extends the control principle already in the law, and does not break with it.

The duty binds the parties who report or rely on capability figures, namely evaluators, integrators, red teams, and procurement testers, not an adversary assembling a harness. Its object is the integrity of the figures on which restriction, procurement, and certification decisions rest.

One might object that the assembler is already covered: whoever "puts into service" an AI system is a provider under the AI Act, and Article twenty-five's substantial-modification rule seems designed for this case. We think the fit is looser than it looks, in three ways.

First, the provider and substantial-modification duties are triggered by placing a system on the market or putting it into service. A harness built to evaluate capability is often used internally and never placed on any market, so the trigger may simply not fire even though the capability is fully realized.

Second, Article twenty-five's substantial-modification path presupposes a high-risk system already on the market that is then modified. But the base here is an open-weights general-purpose model, whose own high-risk classification is contested, so it is unclear there is a qualifying prior system to modify.

Third, the general-purpose-model obligations attach to the model's developer, and the "downstream provider" duties that reach integrators are comparatively light and information-oriented, not framed around a duty to assess elicited offensive capability.

None of this means no rule could ever be stretched to cover the assembler; it means coverage is contested, incidental, and not designed for the assessment duty we are pointing at. That integrators must be argued into or out of each of these categories shows the vocabulary predates the role, which is now where the capability sits.

Why the provider cannot bear it alone

A further reason the duty cannot rest solely on the model provider is epistemic, and it moves the test from control to control plus foreseeability. A provider's capability evaluation is a lower bound, not an upper bound. It measures what the provider's own elicitation, meaning its prompts, its scaffold, and its fine-tuning budget, managed to surface, and a third party with a better harness, more compute, or different intuitions about the model's strengths can exceed it.

Elicitation bounds risk only under strong assumptions, and when they fail the developer's number guarantees less than the model can actually do. Models can even underperform strategically on the evaluations meant to characterize them. This is the epistemic form of our thesis: the provider's assessment is structurally incomplete because capability is elicited, not intrinsic.

This consequence cuts against our own argument, and we address it directly. The elicitation gap is also the best case for access restriction: if a provider cannot bound what others will elicit, gating the model is a rational hedge. We do not argue that away. The same gap makes model-level assessment necessary, since before release it is the provider's only tool. It also makes it insufficient, since the part that escapes the provider's measurement is also the part that escapes the provider's control once the model is out, most of all for open weights. Both halves of the claim follow from the same fact.

The provider still owes something, and it is specific and bounded. It should publish capability as a lower bound with the elicitation conditions disclosed, so downstream actors know what was and was not tested. It should keep monitoring and controls where they still exist, as with closed and API-served models, where the provider can observe elicitation and so owes more. And it should run elicitation testing before release when there will be no control after it, as with open weights.

It also follows that credible assessment cannot be the provider's alone: independent parties must be resourced to run the elicitation the provider cannot, which is a governance case for external evaluation access and for the third-party audit ecosystem more broadly.

Implications for procurement and regulation

The difference between a forty-five point eight percent and a zero percent Effective Harm Rate is a supply-chain risk determination, not a product preference: the chosen model's failure mode under adversarial pressure propagates into every downstream system that embeds it. Yet organizations have no standardized method for evaluating the adversarial robustness of the models they procure, and vendor-published benchmarks can mislead in either direction.

A procurement standard that asked for harm-grounded figures and attribution reporting, with the evaluation code open to audit, would test the two things this paper shows current practice gets wrong. This also bears on the concentration of security capability: if independent evaluation of frontier claims is gated by the very labs whose claims need testing, the accountability horizon closes where it most needs to be open. Open, reproducible harnesses spread the ability to test, and they spread the ability to attack as well. The assessment duty therefore grows more important as the tools spread, not less important.

We do not claim any of this is easy to enforce. A harness is software; anyone with the expertise can write and share one, and no access gate stops that. Our claim is prior to enforcement and does not depend on solving it: whatever a regulator or procurement team acts on, it must first measure correctly, and the instruments in use measure the wrong thing in both directions.

Getting the measurement right, and attaching it to the actor who controls the system, is what any later policy depends on, whether that policy works at the model level or elsewhere. Behind this sits a trade-off the paper does not settle. Offensive capability is currently developed and deployed faster than the regulation and audit tools meant to assess it, and the balance between that pace and safety is being set largely by developers rather than by public bodies, which, as the twenty twenty-six episode showed, are left to improvise under time pressure. Whoever makes that trade-off can only make it well with instruments that measure the right thing.

Objections and responses

First objection: "Access restrictions still buy defensive time, even if they do not bound capability in the limit." We grant the structure and much of the premise: restrictions do buy time, while we dispute the level at which they buy it.

The strongest evidence here is not ours. The UK AI Security Institute's evaluation of a second developer's frontier model, published on the thirtieth of April twenty twenty-six, found comparable expert-level cyber-task scores, seventy-one point four percent for the second model against Mythos's sixty-eight point six percent. It also found a second end-to-end completion of the same thirty-two-step simulation, in two of ten attempts, and suggested that offensive cyber skill may be emerging as a byproduct of more general gains in autonomy, reasoning, and coding.

AgentFlow shows a large harness contribution with an open-weights model and an automated harness-search loop. And the export-control episode itself was reversed in under three weeks. The diffusion has already occurred at the level of capability class. Restrictions on specific models still buy time on specific exploit chains against specific products; they buy less at the level the policy debate is conducted at.

Second objection: "Restricting frontier models still bounds the typical attacker, because effective scaffolds require expertise." This grants our results and rescues restrictions on different grounds. The nine of nine assisted recall reflects months of design iteration, weakness-taxonomy knowledge, AddressSanitizer instrumentation, and the discipline to catch a label-leakage bug, none of which are commodity skills. On this reading the zero of nine autonomous result supports restrictions: without the frontier model, the median attacker has nothing.

The argument's narrowness is its problem. Offensive-AI policy targets state-aligned groups, organized criminal enterprises, and persistent threats, none of whom are scaffold-constrained, and scaffolds become commodity once published. The expertise barrier is real for the median actor and largely irrelevant for the actors the policy is written against.

Third objection: "A single planted target with an iterated framework does not generalize." We grant this without reservation. It is the reason why section five is framed as a case study. The framework was iterated against this target across five versions, and the assisted nine of nine is close to a fixed point of that iteration.

The argument depends not on the nine of nine but on the assisted-versus-autonomous distance at fixed model and target, and the iteration does not manufacture that distance: no amount of scaffold tuning makes the autonomous model recover the bugs. Generalization is the right next experiment: a staged evaluation that reproduces known vulnerabilities at pre-patch commits before testing novel targets under responsible disclosure.

Fourth objection: "Adversarial assemblers will not comply." This is true, and it is why the two are complements rather than substitutes. The duty here is a measurement-integrity obligation on the parties who produce and consume capability figures, not a constraint on attackers, and model-level restriction remains necessary for exactly the actors who will not comply.

Limitations and conclusion

The vulnerability case study used a planted target with known ground truth, not a production codebase. Real-world discovery involves larger codebases, more complex build systems, and no prior knowledge of bug locations. The hundred percent pipeline recall and the zero percent autonomous crash-verified recall are evidence about this specific target under specific configurations, not claims about arbitrary software.

Experiment two holds the model fixed and varies only the scaffold, while experiment one holds the attacking swarm fixed and varies the target model. Neither varies model scale. This isolates the scaffold's contribution in experiment two but says nothing about the converse: we cannot say whether a larger model would close the same gap without a scaffold, because we did not vary the model's size.

We therefore make no claim that systems matter more than models. The claim is narrower: capability, and the measurement of capability, are properties of the system, and model-level figures reported in isolation mislead. A model-size sweep at fixed scaffold is the natural complement.

The experiment one targets, GPT-4o and Claude Sonnet four, were superseded by mid-twenty twenty-six, and we chose not to re-run against current models. The claim we intend to transfer is the divergence between technical success rate and Effective Harm Rate, not the specific magnitudes, which are properties of those models at the time of the runs. Concurrent work reports a related gap between surface safety and jailbreak robustness in small language models, which suggests the divergence is not peculiar to these two targets, but we have not tested current frontier models.

Results depend on hardware, model version, scoring thresholds, and random seeds. Reported numbers are point estimates rather than converged averages, and single-run variance is expected at one point two billion parameters. Reported configurations should be treated as capability floors.

The Effective Harm Rate depends on manual verification, performed by a single annotator in a single pass whose per-response decisions were not logged. It does not scale and yields no measure of inter-annotator agreement, so the absolute rates should be read as one annotator's application of the bar. The gap between targets does not depend on that pass alone: three StrongREJECT graders reproduce it. Because verification covered only the judged-successful responses, the rate can miss harmful outputs the judge scored below threshold, so the reported rates are lower bounds on realized harm, the conservative direction for our argument.

Conclusion. A swarm of one point two billion parameter models running on consumer hardware compromised the output-level safety of one frontier model at a forty-five point eight percent Effective Harm Rate, while extracting no actionable harm from another despite an apparent forty percent jailbreak rate. Embedded in a hand-crafted pipeline, the same swarm recovered nine of nine planted vulnerabilities; run autonomously, it recovered zero of nine by crash verification and two of nine by cited source line.

Two implications follow. First, the categorical safety gap between the two frontier models is not captured by current jailbreak metrics, which score format compliance rather than realized harm. The field lacks a measurement regime reflecting actual stakes, though a frontier developer and its partners have begun drafting one.

Second, on this target a system built around a model orders of magnitude smaller than the frontier models subject to restriction recovers all nine bugs while the same model unaided recovers almost none. This locates the capability, and its cost, in the system rather than in the model alone. A single recall figure blurs the two and, as our own label-leakage correction showed, is easy to inflate.

Access restrictions on frontier models are therefore necessary but not, by themselves, a sufficient security mechanism. What is regulated has to be measured at the level of the system, with metrics that track realized harm, and the duty to measure it has to attach to whoever controls and can foresee what the system does.

Ethical statement

We release the swarm-attack framework on the judgment that the defender's interest in independently evaluating AI systems outweighs the marginal uplift the framework gives attackers. The rest of this section explains how we reached that judgment.

Marginal uplift. The capability classes we demonstrate, coordinated jailbreak search and pipeline-assisted weakness detection, are already attainable by any actor able to read this paper and run a one point two billion parameter model locally. The twenty twenty-six export-control episode is instructive here too: the developer of the restricted model reported that the vulnerabilities at issue were discoverable by other public models without a bypass. These capabilities already exist; our contribution is to measure how cheap they have become and what the scaffold contributes relative to the model. For the median attacker the expertise barrier is real; for the resourced actors offensive-AI policy actually targets, the framework provides little they could not assemble.

Adverse impacts and mitigations. The vulnerability case study uses a synthetic target constructed by the authors; no production codebase is targeted and no real-world vulnerability is disclosed in this paper. Experiment one produced actionable harmful content from one target model; none of it is reproduced in this paper.

So that the manual verification can be audited, the anonymized supplementary material provided for review contains the raw per-attack logs, including target-model responses. The public release will separate the harness, agent roles, attack strategies, verification rubric, and per-attack scores, which will be openly licensed, from the response text of attacks verified as harmful, which will be available to researchers only on request. Experiment one attacks were conducted via standard API endpoints under each provider's terms of service.

The staged generalization study follows responsible-disclosure protocols: pre-patch reproduction of already-fixed vulnerabilities first, then any novel-target work coordinated with affected vendors before publication. The primary near-term users we anticipate are AI labs, security teams, and procurement evaluators who need an open testbed for adversarial-robustness measurement and currently have no viable independent alternative.

Why open rather than gated. We considered a gated or staged release of the framework, vetting requesters or releasing capability in stages, and decided against it for this artifact, though not without reservation. The one exception is the response text of attacks verified as harmful, which we withhold from the open release: it adds nothing to the testbed's defensive value and is the harmful material itself. The case for gating is the elicitation-gap argument, and we take it seriously. Three considerations decided us.

First, the offensive increment is low and the capability class is already diffused. The components are coordination, role differentiation, and evolutionary search over a small open-weights model, all reconstructable from this paper alone, and in the export-control episode the developer reported that other public models found the same bugs without a bypass.

Second, a gate would reproduce the exact dependency the paper criticizes, making independent evaluation of frontier claims contingent on the same institutions whose claims are under test. It would fall hardest on the under-resourced defenders, regulators, and procurement teams who most need an open testbed, while the resourced adversaries that offensive-AI policy targets are the least gate-constrained.

Third, what a gate buys is time, and the defensive value of an open testbed is also time-sensitive.

Reproducibility. The supplementary material ships the raw per-attack logs and result files for both experiments together with audit scripts. The scripts recompute from the stored data and make no model calls, so their output is deterministic. For experiment one, the script reproduces exactly the attack counts, two hundred and twenty-five per target, the technical success rates of sixty point nine percent and forty point zero percent, the perfect-score counts of eighteen and zero, and the per-generation evolution figures.

The Effective Harm Rate, the critical-severity counts, and the refusal rate come from the manual verification pass. We report them as author-verified quantities; the released scripts do not recompute them. The full target responses are included in the released attack libraries, so these judgments can be checked independently against the same data, and we additionally release a script that grades every response with the published StrongREJECT rubric for a reproducible, harm-grounded score alongside the verified rate. Keeping pipeline output separate from author judgment in this way is the discipline section six argues evaluations should adopt.

For experiment two, a second script recomputes the assisted recall at each swarm size, six of nine, eight of nine, and the combined nine of nine as the union of the source and fuzzing phases, and the autonomous crash- and citation-verified recall, zero of nine and two of nine, all from the stored result files.
